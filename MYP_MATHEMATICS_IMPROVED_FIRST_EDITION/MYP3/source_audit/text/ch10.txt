I N Q U I R Y
How can a limited sample support a claim about a whole population —
and how should the uncertainty in that claim be communicated?
AT L · C O M M U N I CAT I O N
A probability is only as good as the assumptions behind it, and a survey is only as good as the people
it reached. Both have to be explained in plain language: what was counted, what was assumed, who
was included and how large the uncertainty is. “One in four” and “25%” can describe the same
number yet leave a reader with different impressions.

KEY AND RELATED CONCEPTS
Logic. Probability reasons from a clearly defined
set of outcomes to a quantified conclusion. The
conclusion is only valid while the assumptions —
a fair die, a representative sample — remain true.
Quantity. Probabilities are numbers between 
and . Expected frequencies scale with the
number of trials, and a sample proportion is an
estimate rather than the population value itself.
Models and justification. A theoretical
probability and a sampling method are both
models of a real process. Evidence for a claim
about a population depends on how the sample
was selected and how the result is reported.
GLOBAL CONTEXT AND EXPLORATION
Identities and relationships. Exploration:
how surveys about young people’s habits,
health risks and school decisions are
sampled, summarised and used — and who
is left out when a sample is chosen badly.
Statistical claims shape how groups are
seen: “most teenagers”, “one in four
students”, “a 90% accurate test”. The
mathematics cannot decide the policy, but
it can show whether the sample represents
the group, whether a risk is stated fairly,
and how much confidence a number
deserves.

CRITERION A · I, II
CRITERION B · I, II, III
JUSTIFICATION
A probability describes a model. A survey describes people. The bridge between them is
the sample: a small group chosen so that what is true of the group is likely to be roughly
true of the whole.

Figure 10.6 A biased sample over-represents one group, while a random sample gives every group a chance. Sample size alone
cannot repair a method that systematically misses part of the population.
Random selection is the strongest defence against bias, but it is not the only decision. A sample also
needs a complete frame: if the list omits students who are absent, or people without a phone, the
“random” draw is random only from the part that could be reached. Three common methods are:
Convenience samples — asking the first 
 people in the queue — and voluntary-response samples —
inviting anyone to vote online — are not random. They are cheap, but they systematically favour
people who are easy to reach or strongly motivated, and no increase in size removes that direction
of error.
10.4
D E F I N I T I O N S
The population is the whole group about which we want information. A sample is the part
we actually observe, and the sample size is the number of individuals in it. A census
measures every member of the population. A random sample gives every member a known,
non-zero chance of being chosen. The sampling frame is the list from which the sample is
drawn. Bias is a systematic tendency for a sample or measurement to differ from the
population in a particular direction. A representative sample has roughly the same
composition as the population on the characteristics that matter. A stratified sample selects
from each subgroup in proportion to its size.
Simple random sample. Number the frame and use a random-number generator to choose
individuals. Every member has the same chance.
Systematic sample. Choose every th name on the frame after a random start. Convenient when the
list is long, provided the list has no repeating pattern that matches .

Stratified sample. Divide the population into groups such as year levels or regions, then sample
within each group in proportion to its size. This guarantees representation of every group.
