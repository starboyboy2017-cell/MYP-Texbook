Data do not speak for themselves. A scatter plot can
suggest a relationship; a correlation coefficient can
measure its strength; neither can establish that one
variable causes another. This part develops the tools of
bivariate analysis and probability, then turns them on
the questions that matter: What does the evidence
support, how was the sample chosen, and how should
uncertainty be communicated?

KEY AND RELATED CONCEPTS
Relationships. Bivariate data records two

Justification. Association is not causation. A

alternative explanations.

measurements move together?”. Each observation contributes a pair, and the pair is

Each point represents one observation: two numerical variables measured on the same individual.
Figure 9.1 Each point is one student: study time and score measured together. The points rise from left to right, so the association is
positive. The highlighted point sits above the pattern for its study time.
Label the axes with the quantity and its unit, and choose scales that use the available space without
distorting the pattern. The explanatory variable is usually placed on the horizontal axis because we
are asking how the response depends on it. When neither variable is clearly prior, either axis may
be used, but the choice must be stated and kept consistent when comparing plots.
Describe a scatter plot in four moves: direction (positive, negative or none), form (linear or
curved), strength (how closely the points follow the form) and unusual features (outliers, clusters
or gaps). “Positive, linear and strong, with one high outlier” is a complete description; “it goes up” is
not.
9.1
D E F I N I T I O N S
Bivariate data consists of two measurements made on the same individual, object or
occasion. The explanatory variable is the one we use to explain or predict; the response
variable is the one we measure as an outcome. A scatter plot displays each pair as a point,
with the explanatory variable on the horizontal axis. An association is a pattern in which the
response tends to change as the explanatory variable changes. An outlier is a point that does
not fit the overall pattern. A cluster is a separated group of points.

 The line of best fit
CRITERION A · II
CRITERION C · III, IV
MODELS
A line of best fit is a model of the trend. It smooths the scatter into a single statement: a
starting value and a rate of change. Its usefulness depends on how well the points fit and
on where the prediction is made.
To draw a line by eye, plot the mean point, choose two points on the line that are far apart, and
check that the number of points above and below the line is roughly balanced. A ruler placed
through the mean point can be rotated until the vertical distances of the points from the line look
as small as possible. The least-squares line makes the sum of the squared residuals as small as
possible; software and spreadsheets compute it directly.

D E F I N I T I O N S
Causation means that a change in one variable produces a change in the other. A
confounding variable is a third variable that influences both the explanatory and response
variables, creating an association that is not due to a direct causal link. Reverse causation
occurs when the response variable actually influences the explanatory variable. An
observational study records variables without intervening; a controlled experiment
deliberately changes the explanatory variable and compares outcomes under otherwise
similar conditions.

A strong correlation between X and Y does not prove X causes Y.
A third variable can drive both. Controlled experiments or additional evidence are needed for causal claims.
Figure 9.5 Ice-cream sales and swimming incidents rise together, but buying ice cream does not cause swimming incidents. Hot
weather is a common cause of both. The correlation is real; the causal direction is not.
The classic alternative explanations are worth checking systematically. Is there a common cause,
such as temperature, income or age? Could the direction be reversed, with the response
influencing the explanatory variable? Could the pattern arise from selection, because the people in
the data chose themselves or were chosen in a non-random way? Could it be a coincidence,
especially in a small sample or when many possible pairs were examined? Could the variables be
measuring the same underlying quantity, such as height and arm span?

KEY IDEAS
Bivariate data pairs two measurements on
the same unit; a scatter plot displays the
pairs.
Describe a scatter plot by direction, form,
strength and unusual features.
Pearson's  measures the direction and
strength of a linear association, from 
 to .
 is unchanged by rescaling or swapping
variables, but it is sensitive to outliers and
blind to curved patterns.
A line of best fit passes through the mean
point and balances residuals; its gradient
and intercept need contextual
interpretation.
Interpolation is safer than extrapolation; a
model should not be extended far beyond
the observed range.
Correlation is not causation: common
causes, reverse causation, selection and
chance are alternative explanations.
A causal claim is supported by controlled
experiments, correct temporal order, a
mechanism and the ruling out of
confounders.
Graphs and headlines can mislead through
truncated axes, relative versus absolute risk,
selection and subgroup reversal.
Always report the sample, the measure, the
range and the uncertainty alongside a
correlation.
