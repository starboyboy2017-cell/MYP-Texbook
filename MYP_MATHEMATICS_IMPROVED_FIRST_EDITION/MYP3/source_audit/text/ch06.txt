I N Q U I R Y
How much information do you need before you can be certain that two
shapes are identical?
AT L · T H I N K I N G T H R O U G H J U S T I F I CAT I O N
A drawing can suggest that two shapes are congruent; it cannot prove it. In this chapter, separate
demonstration from deduction. Describe each transformation precisely — centre, angle, direction,
mirror line — so that a reader could reproduce it without seeing your diagram. When you claim
congruence, name the equal parts that are given and the criterion they satisfy. When a claim fails,
look for the smallest counterexample: one is enough to refute a rule.

KEY AND RELATED CONCEPTS
Form. A shape's form is fixed by its sides, angles
and proportions. Transformations show what is
preserved when a figure moves; congruence
criteria state which measurements fix a triangle
completely.
Space. Position and orientation in the plane.
Coordinates make transformations calculable,
and symmetry describes an object that maps
onto itself.
Justification. Congruence criteria turn measured
facts into deductions; a proof records each step
so that another person can check it.
GLOBAL CONTEXT AND EXPLORATION
Personal and cultural expression.
Exploration: how designers and artisans use
transformations, symmetry and tessellation
to build pattern, and how mathematical
structure limits what repeated tiles can
make.
A woven band, a tiled courtyard and a
printed logo can all be described by the
same three moves: slide, flip, turn.
Mathematics does not explain why a pattern
moves us, but it explains which patterns
continue without gaps and how a repetition
can be specified precisely enough to
reproduce.

 Symmetry and tessellation
CRITERION B · II
CRITERION D · II
PATTERNS
A shape has symmetry when a transformation maps it onto itself. Symmetry is
congruence applied to a single object, and it explains why a pattern can repeat across a
surface without gaps or overlaps.
For a regular polygon with  sides, the two symmetry numbers agree: there are  lines of symmetry
and rotational symmetry of order . For other shapes they can differ. A non-square rectangle has
two lines of symmetry and rotational symmetry of order 2; a parallelogram that is not a rectangle
has no lines of symmetry but still has rotational symmetry of order 2; an isosceles triangle has one
line of symmetry and no rotational symmetry.
