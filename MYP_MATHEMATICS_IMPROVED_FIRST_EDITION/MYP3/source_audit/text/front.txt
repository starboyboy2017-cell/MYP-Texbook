O R I E N TAT I O N
How to use this coursebook
Year 3 is where several familiar ideas become tools for general reasoning.
A letter starts to stand for a family of numbers, a table of values becomes a
relationship, a drawing becomes evidence for a claim, and a set of
measurements becomes an argument about uncertainty.
You already know how to calculate with whole
numbers, fractions and decimals. You have
met negative numbers, simple equations and
the basic properties of shapes. This course
asks you to work with those ideas at a different
level: to explain why a rule always works, to
choose an efficient representation, to connect
an equation with its graph, and to decide when
a numerical answer is precise enough to be
useful.
The book is arranged in four parts. Part I
builds the number system and algebraic
structure that the rest of the course depends
on: irrational numbers, exponent laws,
scientific notation, functions and algebraic
manipulation. Part II turns that structure into
relationships: gradient and intercept, linear
models, equations, inequalities and systems.
Part III is geometry as a deductive discipline:
transformations, congruence, similarity, right-
triangle trigonometry, and the measurement
of solids. Part IV examines data and chance,
with particular attention to the difference
between a pattern in data and a causal claim.
Every chapter follows the same inquiry
rhythm. A situation raises a question;
definitions and representations give you the
tools to investigate it; worked examples make
the reasoning visible; graded practice makes
the method dependable; an investigation, an
application and a criterion task ask you to use
the mathematics beyond the original example.
The answer section confirms your reasoning
rather than replacing it.

T H E P R O G R A M M E I N P R A C T I C E
The MYP framework in this course
The Middle Years Programme asks students to learn mathematics as a
connected, conceptual and applicable discipline. Concepts organise the
content, contexts give it purpose, and criteria make the quality of thinking
visible.
Aims of MYP mathematics
The MYP mathematics framework aims to help students enjoy mathematics, develop curiosity and
confidence, build conceptual understanding, apply mathematics to authentic situations,
communicate reasoning, think critically and creatively, and appreciate the international and
historical dimensions of the subject. In Year 3, the emphasis shifts from describing procedures to
explaining structure: why exponent laws follow from repeated multiplication, why a linear
function has constant gradient, why congruence can be established from limited information, and
why correlation alone cannot establish causation.

Inquiry in a mathematics lesson
Inquiry does not mean that students are left to rediscover every definition without guidance. It
means that a question genuinely drives the sequence of work, that students make and test
conjectures, and that they can explain how a result was reached. The course uses a repeating
pathway.

Assessment criteria in practice
MYP mathematics uses four criteria. They describe different qualities of
mathematical work, not four separate syllabuses. A single rich task can
provide evidence for more than one criterion.
How the criteria work together
A criterion task in this book names the criteria it is designed to evidence. A task may, for example,
ask you to model a situation (A), identify and generalise a pattern (B), present the reasoning (C) and
evaluate the accuracy and meaning of the result (D). Not every exercise needs all four criteria.
Short fluency exercises usually provide evidence for A only; an investigation may provide evidence
for B and C; a modelling task may provide evidence for A, C and D.

KNOWING AND UNDERSTANDING
Select appropriate mathematics for

successfully.
Solve problems correctly across a range of
contexts.
In this course: number systems, exponent

WHAT TO SHOW
WHAT STRONG WORK LOOKS LIKE
Selection
The chosen method fits the structure of the problem, and an alternative
was considered where relevant.
Application
Each step is mathematically valid, notation is accurate, and the result is
checked against the context.
Justification
Claims are supported by reasons, definitions or evidence; a generalisation
is distinguished from a set of examples.
Communication
A reader can follow the reasoning without reconstructing missing steps;
diagrams and units are purposeful.
Evaluation
Limitations, assumptions and the appropriateness of the accuracy are
stated, not merely implied.
Formative practice is not a formal assessment
Most questions in this book are practice, not summative assessment. Their purpose is to produce
feedback: an error is information about what to practise next. Criterion tasks at the end of each
chapter are longer and are accompanied by a planning frame and a course rubric. Teachers should
apply their school’s official assessment descriptors and procedures; the bands in this book are
teaching summaries, not official IB level descriptors.
A N OT E O N C O M M A N D T E R M S
State requires a short answer without justification. Describe requires relevant features.
Explain requires reasons or mechanisms. Justify requires evidence or a valid argument.
Evaluate requires a judgment supported by criteria. Investigate requires a planned process,
data or examples, a pattern, and a conclusion. The command term tells you how much
reasoning to show, not how long the answer must be.

How the parts build on one another
Part I
Structure before method
Irrational numbers extend the number line;
exponent laws compress repeated
multiplication; algebraic notation names a
function. Without this structure, later
procedures are memorised rather than
understood.
Part II
Relationships as objects
Linear functions connect tables, graphs,
equations and contexts. Solving equations and
systems is then a question about intersection
and balance, not a sequence of arbitrary
moves.
Part III
Reasoning about space
Transformations make congruence precise.
Similarity makes trigonometric ratios possible.
Solids turn those ratios into volume and
surface area, including the surprising cube law
for scaling.
Part IV
Evidence and uncertainty
Bivariate data connects algebra and statistics.
Probability quantifies uncertainty. Sampling
explains why a claim from data needs a
method, a margin of doubt and a causal
argument.

Writing a solution
A complete solution does not need to be long. It needs to show the selection of a method, the
execution of that method, and a check or conclusion. In this course, the pattern is:
1
Given and required. What information is supplied, and what must be found or shown?
2
Method. Which definition, rule or representation applies, and why?
3
Working. Carry out the steps without skipping a transformation that changes meaning.
4
Answer and check. State the answer with units, then test it against the context or by an
independent method.

memorise more rules, but to see why the rules are forced
