The real number system
A number line drawn with a ruler looks continuous,
yet the numbers that fill it are of strikingly different
kinds. Some can be written as exact fractions; some
cannot be written as fractions at all. Understanding
that difference changes what “exact” means.

KEY AND RELATED CONCEPTS
Form. A number has more than one possible
written form: fraction, terminating decimal,
repeating decimal, surd or approximation. The
form reveals which operations are exact and
which are limited.
Quantity. The real number line assigns a position
to every decimal, including those with infinitely
many digits. Order and distance can be described
without listing every digit.
Equivalence. Different decimals, fractions and
radical expressions can name the same real
number. Recognising equivalence is central to
simplifying and to proving.
GLOBAL CONTEXT AND EXPLORATION
Orientation in space and time. Exploration:
how different cultures represented and
approximated quantities, and why the
discovery of incommensurable lengths
challenged the idea that all lengths could be
expressed as whole-number ratios.
Greek mathematicians initially assumed
that any two lengths could be compared
using a common unit. The diagonal of a
square exposed a length with no such
common unit. That discovery did not break
mathematics; it forced a broader
conception of number. Today, irrational
numbers appear in geometry, music theory,
engineering tolerances and computer
graphics.

A rational number is a quotient of two integers. That simple definition unifies whole
numbers, fractions, terminating decimals and repeating decimals: they are different
