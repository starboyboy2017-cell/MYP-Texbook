A carton, a can and a water tank are all answers to the
same question: how much space must be enclosed,
and how much material will enclose it? Unfold the
surface, slice the solid, fill it with layers, and the
answer becomes a calculation.

How much material does it take to hold a given volume — and how

AT L · S E L F - M A N AG E M E N T A N D C R I T I CA L T H I N K I N G

hide inside a long chain of arithmetic.

KEY AND RELATED CONCEPTS
Form. A solid is defined by its faces, edges and
vertices. A net, a set of views or a cross-section is
another representation of the same form, chosen
to make a measurement visible.
Measurement. Surface area measures the
boundary in square units; volume measures
enclosed space in cubic units. Both depend on
length, but differently, and must be reported in
matching units.
Models and change. A formula models an ideal
solid; real cans have seams, rims and varied wall
thickness. Scaling changes area and volume at
different rates, so large containers use less
material per litre.
GLOBAL CONTEXT AND EXPLORATION
Globalization and sustainability.
Exploration: packaging and material use —
how the shape of a container affects the
material consumed, the energy needed to
transport it, and the waste left behind.
A drink can is a compromise. A taller,
thinner can uses less metal for the same
volume, but it may be less stable, harder to
hold and more expensive to ship.
Mathematical optimisation does not make
the decision; it makes the trade-offs visible
and quantifiable.

FORM
A solid is a three-dimensional region, but its surface can be described by counting and
naming its parts. Those counts are not bookkeeping: for a whole family of solids they obey
a fixed relationship, and that relationship is a first example of a structural theorem.

The names of a prism and a pyramid come from their bases. A triangular prism has triangular
bases; a pentagonal pyramid has a pentagonal base. The orientation of the solid does not change its
name: a triangular prism standing on a rectangular face is still a triangular prism, and its
triangular faces are still the bases used in the volume formula.

D E F I N I T I O N S
A polyhedron is a closed solid whose faces are polygons. A face is a flat polygonal surface, an
edge is a segment where two faces meet, and a vertex is a point where edges meet. A prism
has two congruent parallel bases joined by rectangular faces; a pyramid has one polygonal
base and triangular faces meeting at an apex. Cylinders, cones and spheres have curved
surfaces, so they are not polyhedra. A net is a flat arrangement of faces that folds into a solid.
A plan is the view from directly above; an elevation is a view from the front or the side,
looking horizontally.
