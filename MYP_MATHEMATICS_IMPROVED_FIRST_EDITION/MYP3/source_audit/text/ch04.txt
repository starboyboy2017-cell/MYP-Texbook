Linear relationships and
graphs
Some quantities change at a steady rate. Equal steps
in one produce equal steps in the other, and a graph
makes that regularity visible as a straight line. The
line is not just a picture: it is a rule for predicting
beyond the data.

AT L · C O M M U N I CAT I N G W I T H R E P R E S E N TAT I O N S
A graph is an argument, not decoration. Always label both axes with quantities and units, choose a
scale that makes the relationship readable, and mark key points such as intercepts. When you read a
gradient aloud, name both units: “18 kilometres per hour”, not “18”. A reader should be able to
reconstruct your model from your table, graph and equation without guessing.

KEY AND RELATED CONCEPTS
Relationships. A linear relationship connects
two quantities by a constant rate of change. The
same relationship can be represented by a table,
a graph, an equation or a description in words.
Change. Gradient measures how quickly the
dependent quantity changes as the independent
quantity increases by one unit. A zero gradient
and an undefined gradient describe very
different situations.
Representation. Each representation exposes
different information. A table shows individual
values, a graph shows shape and direction, and
an equation gives the exact rule.
GLOBAL CONTEXT AND EXPLORATION
Fairness and development. Exploration:
how linear cost models can make charges
predictable, and how a fixed fee, a rate or a
cap affects different users unequally.
Two businesses may describe their prices
with straight lines that look equally simple.
The intercept says how much someone pays
before using anything; the gradient says
how much each additional unit costs.
Reading those two numbers together is a
first step in judging whether a price is fair.

CRITERION A · I

REPRESENTATION
A graph is a correspondence between algebra and geometry. Each ordered pair becomes a
point, and a collection of points can reveal a pattern that the numbers alone might hide.
In a linear relationship, the quantity we choose is usually called the independent variable and is
plotted on the horizontal axis. The quantity that responds is the dependent variable and is plotted
on the vertical axis. Time is often independent because we choose when to measure; distance, cost
or height is often dependent because it responds to time or to the amount used.
A table of values is a list of ordered pairs. To plot 
, substitute a few values of , calculate
the corresponding values of , and plot the resulting pairs. Because the rule is linear, two correctly
plotted points would determine the line, but a third point is a useful check.

 Gradient as a rate of change
CRITERION A · II

CHANGE
Gradient is the central measurement of a linear relationship. It answers a practical
question: how much does the dependent quantity change for each additional unit of the
independent quantity?
A positive gradient means the line rises from left to right. A negative gradient means it falls. A
horizontal line has gradient 0 because the vertical change is 0. A vertical line has an undefined
gradient because the horizontal change is 0 and division by zero is undefined. “No slope” is not the
same as “slope zero”.

CRITERION D · I, II, III

MODELS
A linear model is a deliberately simple description of a relationship. Its value lies in
prediction and comparison, but its simplicity also limits what it can claim.
To build a linear model, name the variables and units, identify the fixed amount as the intercept
and the repeated amount as the gradient, then write 
. In a cost model, the intercept is the
amount paid before any use and the gradient is the marginal cost of one more unit. Comparing two
models means comparing both numbers: a lower intercept favours light users, while a lower
gradient favours heavy users.
