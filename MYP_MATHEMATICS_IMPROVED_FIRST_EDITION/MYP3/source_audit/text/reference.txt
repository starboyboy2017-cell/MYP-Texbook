Glossary
Working definitions of the terms used in this course. Where a term has a precise technical meaning,
the definition states the condition the term depends on.
absolute risk — a risk stated as a
number out of a group, such as 3 in
1,000.
absolute value — the distance of a
number from zero; written 
, always
non-negative.
addition rule —
for two events.
angle of depression — the angle
measured downwards from the
horizontal to a line of sight.
angle of elevation — the angle
measured upwards from the
horizontal to a line of sight.
arithmetic sequence — a sequence
with a constant difference between
consecutive terms.
base rate — the prevalence of a
condition in the group tested; it
shapes what a positive result means.
bias — a systematic tendency for a
sample or measurement to differ from
the population in one direction.
bivariate data — data recorded as
pairs of values, so that a relationship
between two variables can be
examined.
capacity — the amount a container
can hold, measured in litres or
millilitres.
causation — a relationship in which a
change in one variable produces a
change in another; it is not
established by correlation alone.
census — a survey that measures
every member of a population.
coefficient of determination — 
,
the proportion of the variation in one
variable explained by the linear
model.
complement (probability) — the
event that  does not occur, written
; 
.
complement (sets) — the elements of
the universal set that are not in the
given set.
composite solid — a solid made by
joining two or more simpler solids.
compound event — an event formed
by combining two or more simple
events.
cone — a solid with a circular base
and a single vertex; its volume is
.
confounding variable — a variable
related to both others in a study,
which can create a misleading
association.
congruent — identical in shape and
size; corresponding lengths and
angles are equal.
conjecture — a statement believed to
be true on the evidence so far but not
yet proved.
continuous data — data that can take
any value in an interval, such as a
length or a time.
convenience sample — a sample
chosen because it is easy to reach; not
random and usually biased.
converse — the statement formed by
swapping the condition and the
conclusion.
correlation — the strength and
direction of a linear association
between two variables.
correlation coefficient — the
number , between 
 and ,
measuring the fit of a linear
relationship.
corresponding — matching in
position or role between two figures
or two sets of data.
counterexample — a single case that
shows a general statement is false.
cross-section — the shape seen when
a solid is cut parallel to its end.
cube root — the number that,
multiplied by itself three times, gives
the original number.
dependent events — events whose
probabilities change when one of
them occurs.
direct proportion — a relationship of

domain — the set of values that may
be used as the input of a function.
ecological fallacy — drawing
conclusions about individuals from
grouped data.
enlargement — a transformation that
multiplies every distance from a
centre by the same scale factor.
equally likely — outcomes with the
same probability, justified by the
physical setup rather than by
counting.
Euler’s formula — for a convex

false negative — a negative test result
for someone who has the condition.
false positive — a positive test result
for someone who does not have the
condition.
fair — describing a die, coin or
spinner whose outcomes are equally
likely.
function — a rule that assigns exactly
one output to each input in the
domain.
gambler’s fallacy — the mistaken
belief that past independent trials

linear equation — an equation in

net — a flat pattern that folds to make
a solid.
non-response — failure of selected
individuals to reply to a survey, a
common source of bias.
order of magnitude — the nearest
power of ten to a quantity.
outcome — one possible result of a
probability experiment.
outlier — a value that lies far from the
general pattern of the data.
percentage point — a unit for the
difference between two percentages.
polyhedron — a solid whose faces are
flat polygons.
population — the whole group about
which information is wanted.
positive predictive value — the
proportion of positive results that are
true positives.
precision — how close repeated
measurements are to each other.
prevalence — the proportion of a
population that has a condition at a
given time.
prism — a solid with a constant cross-
section along its length.
probability — a number between 0
and 1 measuring how likely an event
is.
proof — a chain of reasoning that
establishes a statement beyond doubt
from agreed starting points.
proof by contradiction — a proof
that assumes the opposite of the claim
and shows it leads to an impossibility.
range (function) — the set of values
that a function produces as outputs.
range (statistics) — the largest value
in a data set minus the smallest.
random sample — a sample in which
every member of the population has a
known, non-zero chance of selection.
rational number — a number that
can be written as a fraction of two
integers.
reflection — a transformation that
flips a shape across a line without
changing lengths or angles.
regression to the mean — the
tendency of extreme measurements
to be followed by less extreme ones.
relative frequency — the number of
times an outcome occurred divided
by the number of trials.
relative risk — one risk divided by
another, used to compare them as a
ratio.
representative — describing a
sample with roughly the same
composition as the population.
residual — the observed value minus
the value predicted by a model.
response variable — the variable
plotted on the vertical axis, measured
in response to the other.
risk — the probability of an unwanted
outcome.
rotation — a transformation that
turns a shape about a fixed point by a
given angle.
rotational symmetry — a shape has
rotational symmetry if some rotation
maps it onto itself.
sample — the part of a population
that is actually observed.
sample space — the set of all possible
outcomes of a probability
experiment.
sampling frame — the list from
which a sample is drawn.
sampling variation — the differences
between estimates from different
random samples of the same
population.
scatter plot — a graph plotting paired
bivariate data as points.
scientific notation — a number
written as 
 with 
.
self-selection — bias caused by
individuals choosing whether to take
part.
sensitivity — the proportion of
people with a condition that a test
correctly identifies.
significant figures — the digits of a
number that carry meaning about its
precision.
similar — having the same shape but
not necessarily the same size.
simulation — a model that imitates a
probability experiment, often using
random digits.
simultaneous equations — two or
more equations that must be satisfied
at the same time.
solid — a three-dimensional shape
with faces, edges and vertices.
specificity — the proportion of people
without a condition that a test
correctly clears.
sphere — the set of points at a fixed
distance from a centre; its volume is
.
standard form — another name for
scientific notation, 
.
stratified sample — a sample that
selects from each subgroup in
proportion to its size.
subset — a set whose elements all
belong to a larger set.
surd — an expression containing a
root of a number that is not a perfect
power, such as 
.
surface area — the total area of all the
faces of a solid.
systematic sample — a sample
formed by taking every th member
of a frame after a random start.
tangent — the trigonometric ratio
opposite divided by adjacent; also a
line touching a curve at one point.
tessellation — a tiling of the plane by
shapes with no gaps or overlaps.
theorem — a mathematical statement
that has been proved.
transformation — a change in the
position, size or orientation of a
shape.
translation — a transformation that
slides every point the same distance
in the same direction.
tree diagram — a diagram showing
the stages of a multi-stage experiment
and their probabilities.
trial — one repetition of a probability
experiment.
two-way table — a table recording
counts for two categorical variables at
once.
union — 
, the outcomes

 or both.

validity — the extent to which a
method measures what it claims to
measure.
Venn diagram — a diagram using
overlapping regions to show sets and
their relationships.
vertex — a corner of a solid or
polygon where edges meet.
volume — the space occupied by a
solid, measured in cubic units.
voluntary-response sample — a
