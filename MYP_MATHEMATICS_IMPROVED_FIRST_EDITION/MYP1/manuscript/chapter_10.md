Three answers to one question
“What is typical?” has at least three mathematical answers: the mode
(most common), the median (middle in order) and the mean (share out
equally). They answer different questions and can disagree.
10.1 Collecting and organising data
Data vocabulary
Data are observations. A variable is what is being recorded; it is categor-
ical if it names a group and numerical if it is a number. Discrete data can
be counted exactly; continuous data are measured and depend on the
accuracy of the instrument.
Question Variable Type
What is your favourite subject? subject categorical
How many siblings have you? number of siblings numerical, discrete
How tall are you? height numerical, continuous
How do you travel to school? mode of travel categorical
A good survey question has one meaning. “How much screen time do
you have?” can be answered in hours, minutes, or by device, and the
answers will not be comparable. Before collecting data, decide the
unit and the categories.

1 List the possible values: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4.
2 Count each: 0 appears twice, 1 three times, 2 four times, 3 twice, 4 once.
3 Check the frequencies add to 12, the number of matches.
2 + 3 + 4 + 2 + 1 = 12 ✓

10.2 Averages and range
Mean, median, mode and range
Mean = total ÷ number of values. Median = the middle value of the
ordered list. Mode = the most frequent value. Range = largest − smallest,
a measure of spread, not of average.

P R O B L E M Find the mean, median and mode of 4, 7, 7, 9, 13.
1 Mean: (4 + 7 + 7 + 9 + 13) ÷ 5 = 40 ÷ 5 = 8.
2 Median: the ordered list is unchanged; the middle value is 7.
3 Mode: 7 appears twice, more than any other value.
4 Range: 13 − 4 = 9.
A N S W E R Mean 8, median 7, mode 7, range 9.
The mean is larger than the median because 13 pulls it upward ✓
P R O B L E M Find the median of 3, 5, 8, 10, 12, 15.
1 There is no single middle value with six numbers.
2 The two middle values are 8 and 10.
3 Median = (8 + 10) ÷ 2 = 9.
Three values lie below 9 and three above ✓
Taking the median of an unordered list
The median must be found after ordering. In 10, 2, 6, 4 the middle of the
written list is 6, but the ordered list is 2, 4, 6, 10 and the median is (4 + 6)
÷ 2 = 5.

P R O B L E M A pie chart shows 25 % walking, 40 % by bus, 20 % by car and the rest by bicycle. How many students cycle in a school of 480?
1 The four shares must total 100 %: 25 + 40 + 20 = 85, so cycling is 15 %.
2 15 % of 480 = 0.15 × 480 = 72.
3 Check: 120 + 192 + 96 + 72 = 480.
The four numbers reproduce the whole school ✓
A pie chart of a total that means nothing
Pie charts require parts of one whole. A pie chart of “number of goals
per player” is meaningless once you realise the goals are not a fixed total
being shared out.

10.4 Interpreting graphs critically
Every graph contains choices: which axis, which scale, where the ver-
tical axis starts, which summary is quoted. A reader who checks those
choices can tell the difference between a real change and a drawn
one.

Four questions to ask of any graph
Check What to look for
The axes What is on each axis, and in what unit?
The scale Does the vertical axis start at zero? Are the steps equal?
The sample How many observations, and who was asked?
The summary Which average is quoted, and why that one?
P R O B L E M A shop advertises “average customer spending of 45” after recording spending of 8, 9, 10, 12, 160. Is the claim fair?
1 Total = 8 + 9 + 10 + 12 + 160 = 199, so mean = 39.8 — not 45.
2 The 160 is an outlier and pulls the mean upward.
3 The median is 10, which represents four of the five customers far better.
4 The claim is arithmetically close but statistically misleading.
A N S W E R Not fair: the median is 10; the mean is distorted by one outlier.
Four of five customers spend under 13 ✓

The result
The mean uses every value, so a single extreme value can move it a long way. The median depends only on posi-
tion, so it barely moves. Neither is “correct”: the choice depends on what the summary is for.

What this chapter established
– Data are classified by type before they are displayed.
– The mean, median and mode answer different versions of “typical”.
– The range describes spread, not average.
– Bar charts compare categories, dot plots show shape, pie charts show shares.
– A graph's scale and a summary's choice can change the claim it makes.
Vocabulary
Term Meaning
data Observations collected for a purpose.
variable The quantity or category being recorded.
frequency How many times a value occurs.
mean Total divided by the number of values.
median The middle value of an ordered list.
mode The most frequent value.
range Largest value minus smallest value.
outlier A value far from the rest of the data.
Misconceptions to watch
Three that persist
– The mean is the typical value. With an outlier it may describe nobody in the set.
– The range is an average. It measures spread, not centre.
– A pie chart suits any data. It needs parts of one whole.

Closing the course
Nine chapters ago a number was a written symbol. It is now a position, a power, a factor, a fraction, a percentage, a
variable, a length, an area, a volume and finally a data point — and each of those is a choice about what to notice.
Mathematics is the discipline of making those choices explicit enough that someone else can check them.
