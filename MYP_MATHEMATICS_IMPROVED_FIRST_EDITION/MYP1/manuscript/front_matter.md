This is a working book. Expect to write in the margins, test an idea, compare your meth- od with a classmate's, and return to a question after your thinking has changed.
Year 1 is where many familiar ideas become precise. You already know how numbers are written, how
shapes are named and how data can be displayed. This course asks you to look underneath those familiar
forms: why a digit's position changes its value, why equivalent fractions name the same quantity, why an
angle fact must hold for every triangle rather than merely one drawing.
The book is arranged in five parts. Part I establishes the structure of the number system and the rules that
keep calculation reliable. Part II develops fractions, decimals and percentages as connected ways of describ-
ing parts of a whole. Part III introduces algebraic thinking through patterns and balance. Part IV brings to-
gether sets, shape and measurement. Part V examines data, graphs and the claims people make from them.
Every chapter follows one rhythm, and the rhythm is visible on the page: Engage, Explore, Explain, Worked
Examples, Practice, Investigate and Apply, Reflect and Consolidate. The stage labels tell you where you are,
so that a lesson can be planned around a stage rather than a page number.
Stage What you do What it is for
01 Engage Meet a situation, photograph or puzzle. Give the mathematics a reason to exist.
02 Explore Try something before being told the rule. Build a conjecture you can test.
03 Explain Read definitions, notation and properties. Turn the conjecture into a structure you can use.
04 Worked examples Follow reasoning line by line, including checks. See what a complete answer looks like.
05 Practice Work from fluency to application and extension. Make the method reliable and portable.
06 Investigate and apply Use mathematics on a real task. Meet the MYP criteria in a genuine setting.
07 Reflect Summarise, check vocabulary, self-assess. Know precisely what you can and cannot yet do.

Three habits that make the book work
Estimate first. Predict the size of an answer before calculating it. Ask
what a symbol means. Every letter, bracket and index is doing a job.
Write the check. A calculation you cannot check is a calculation you do
not yet trust.
Writing in this book
The wide outer margin is part of the page, not decoration. Use it for a
question you could not finish, a note that a method reminded you of
something, or the reason a step works. When you return to a chapter
before an assessment, the margin is the fastest way back into your
own thinking.
When you are stuck What to do before asking
The question is unread- able
Underline the noun the question is asking about; the verb is usually the operation.
The method is forgotten Read the most recent worked example in the same section and copy its first line.
The answer looks wrong Estimate the size first, then check the units and the last step.
The question is ambigu- ous
Write down both readings and say which one you are answering.

Inquiry
Each chapter opens with a statement of inquiry, one factual, one conceptual and one debatable question,
and a pathway that shows how the inquiry develops. A factual question has a settled answer. A conceptual
question requires you to connect ideas. A debatable question has more than one defensible answer and must
be argued.
ATL skills in this course
Approaches to learning are written in the form the MYP uses: “In order for a student to …, students must …”. Every
chapter contains at least two ATL boxes: one for a thinking skill and one for a self-management, communication or
research skill.

MYP mathematics uses four criteria. They describe different qualities of mathematical work, not four separate syllabuses: a single rich task can provide evidence for more than one criterion.
Criterion What it looks for Typical evidence in this book
A · Knowing and understand- ing
Selecting and applying the right mathemat- ics, accurately.
Practice sets, chapter reviews, fluency items.
B · Investigating patterns Recognising, describing and justifying a pat- tern, then generalising it.
The numbered investigations, extension items.
C · Communicating Using notation, language and representations precisely, for an audience.
Explanations, diagrams, mini-tasks written for a named reader.
D · Applying mathematics Using mathematics in a real context and judging whether the answer is reasonable.
Real-life applications and criterion-mapped mini-tasks.
Command words that shape the evidence
Command word What a complete answer contains
State The value, name or fact, with no working required.
Describe What happens, in order, with the key features named.
Explain A reason, connected to a property, definition or example.
Justify Evidence: a calculation, a counterexample or a diagram.
Show that A chain of working that ends at the stated result.
Investigate Cases, a pattern, a general rule and a test of that rule.
Evaluate A judgement with stated criteria and a limitation.
Formative practice is not a formal assessment
The exercises in this book build the understanding that assessed tasks later require. Marks are not the point of a
practice set; a question you cannot yet do is information, not a verdict.

How the four criteria accumulate
Criterion A grows as the toolkit grows: by Chapter 10 you are selecting among many methods rather than applying
one. Criterion B appears in every investigation, from counting strings of digits to the relationship between a
cylinder's radius and its volume. Criterion C is practised in every written explanation and every diagram. Criterion
D appears in each chapter's application, where the answer has to be judged as well as calculated.

Five habits that protect accuracy
– Align equal signs. Write one equals sign beneath another so that each line states a true equality.
– Carry units. A number without its unit is an incomplete answer in any measurement question.
– Estimate first. Write the estimate before the calculation, then compare.
– Show the check. Substitute back, reverse the operation, or test with a simpler case.
– Name the rounding. Say “to the nearest hundred” rather than silently changing the value.
ATL · Self-management
In order for a student to keep a page of working readable, students must decide the layout before starting: number
the question, align the equals signs, leave one line between parts, and box the final answer.
Reading a mathematics question
Read it twice. The first reading finds the quantities; the second finds the question. Underline the instruction — cal-
culate, estimate, explain, justify — because the instruction decides what a complete answer contains.
