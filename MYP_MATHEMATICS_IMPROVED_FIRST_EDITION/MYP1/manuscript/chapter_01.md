The whole chapter in one view: concepts, context and the evidence that will be looked for.
Statement of inquiry A number's written form is an organised system of positions: a small set of digits can represent any magnitude when place and zero are used consistently, and the way we record a number decides how precisely we can compare, calculate and communicate it.
Key and related concepts
Key concept · Form. A number's written form is not one symbol but an organised arrangement of positions; chan-
ging a position changes the quantity it records.
Related · Quantity. Place value lets ten digits stand for any size, while rounding records an appropriate level of pre-
cision.
Related · Representation. The same value can appear as words, digits, an expanded sum, a point on a number line
or a rounded claim.
Global context · Orientation in space and time
Exploration: how different cultures have recorded quantity, and why the invention of a place-value system with
zero changed what numbers could do. Every tally system works; only a positional system scales.
Inquiry questions
– Factual: What is the value of a digit in a given position, and how does multiplying or dividing by ten
change a number's digits?
– Conceptual: Why is zero essential in a place-value system, and how can one number have several equival-
ent representations?
– Debatable: When a number is reported as “about 2 000”, is the result more useful than the exact count?

A morning at the market, and a noticeboard that has to be read by everybody.
The trader at the next stall has three boxes of tomatoes: one labelled
“120”, one labelled “1 200” and one labelled “12.00”. A customer reads
all three as “twelve”. She is wrong about at least two of them, and the
trader loses money either way. Nothing about the digits changed.
Only their positions did.
That small scene is the whole chapter. Our number system does not
invent a new symbol for every size. It reuses ten symbols and lets pos-
ition do the work. Understand position, and you can read any number
in the market, on a meter, on a bank statement or on a medicine
bottle.
A market stall organises quantity before anyone writes a number: produce is stacked in rectangular arrays, weights are nested by size, and the counting frame records position. Place value formalises what the stall already does.

What you should have noticed
The digits are unchanged in every case. Only their positions move. Mul-
tiplying by 10 moves every digit one place to the left; dividing by 10
moves every digit one place to the right. The decimal point stays where
it is — it marks the boundary between whole values and parts of one.
1.1 Number systems and the number line
A number line turns “which is bigger?” into a question about position. Everything else in this chapter is a way of writing down what the line already shows.
Mathematicians keep several number families because each one an-
swers a different question. The counting numbers tell you how many
objects there are. Add zero and you can describe an empty shelf. Add
the negatives and you can describe a debt, a temperature below freez-
ing, or a depth below sea level.
The number families
The natural numbers are the counting numbers 1, 2, 3, 4, … The whole
numbers are the natural numbers together with zero: 0, 1, 2, 3, … The in-
tegers are the whole numbers and their negatives: …, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3,
…

A number line is more than a display device. It gives a shared order to
every integer, fraction and decimal. To compare two values, locate
both: the one further to the right is greater. That rule never fails,
which is why it is worth carrying a mental number line for the rest of
your mathematical life.
Reading the symbols
The symbols < and > describe position, not size alone. “3 < 8” is correct
because 3 lies to the left of 8. “−8 < −3” is also correct — even though 8 is
greater than 3 — because −8 lies further left. Reversing the symbol re-
verses the claim.
Reversing a sign when comparing negatives
A student writes −8 > −3 because 8 > 3. On the number line −8 sits to the
left of −3, so −8 < −3. Check every negative comparison against a sketch
of the line, not against the size of the digits.
Working with the line
– Distance from zero is the magnitude. −8 and 8 are equally far from
zero; they are different numbers because their directions differ.
– Between any two values there is another value. Between 3 and 4
lies 3.5; between 3.5 and 3.6 lies 3.55. This is why decimals can al-
ways be refined.
– Inequalities read left to right like a sentence: −5 < −2 < 0 < 3.

Place value
In a base-ten number, the value of a digit is the digit multiplied by ten
raised to the power of its position from the decimal point. The number 3
048.76 therefore means 3 × 1000 + 0 × 100 + 4 × 10 + 8 × 1 + 7 × 0.1 + 6 ×
0.01.
Why zero is not “nothing”
Compare 305, 35 and 3.05. All three use the digits 3 and 5 only. The
zero in 305 keeps the 3 in the hundreds column and the 5 in the ones
column, forcing an empty tens column. In 3.05 the zero holds the
tenths column open so that the 5 lands in the hundredths. Remove the
zero and the number changes completely — the digits have not
changed, but every value they carry has.
Deleting an “unimportant” zero
Writing 4.5 for 4.05, or 4 500 for 4 005, is a place-value error, not a slip of
the pen. Test any suspicious zero by asking: which column does it hold
open? If deleting it moves another digit, the zero is doing work.

1.4 Comparing, ordering and rounding
Comparing decimals by digit length is the most common place-value
error. 0.65 is not larger than 0.7 merely because it has more digits:
0.65 means six tenths and five hundredths; 0.7 means seven tenths.
Line the decimal points up, fill empty columns with zeros, and com-
pare like with like.

<!-- printed page 13 -->
A reliable comparison routine
1. Write both numbers with the decimal points aligned, filling empty
columns with zeros: 0.70 and 0.65. 2. Compare the columns from left to
right. 3. Stop at the first difference — that column decides. Here 7 > 6 in
the tenths column, so 0.70 > 0.65.

Rounding
To round to a stated place, find the digit in that place and look at the di-
git immediately to its right. If that digit is 5 or more, increase the place
digit by 1; otherwise leave it. Replace every digit to the right of the place
with zeros (or drop them after the decimal point).

1.5 Estimation and sensible accuracy
An estimate is not a guess. It is a deliberately simpler calculation
whose answer is close enough to be useful and rough enough to be
done in your head. Estimates do real work: they tell you whether a cal-
culator answer is plausible before you trust it.

Round, calculate, adjust
Round each number to one significant figure, do the simplified calcula-
tion, then adjust for the direction of your rounding. 48 × 21 ≈ 50 × 20 = 1
000. Because both numbers were rounded up, the exact answer must be
a little below 1 000: 1 008.

Reporting more precision than you have
A student measures a desk as 1.2 m and reports its area as 1.44 m². The
measurement is accurate to the nearest 0.1 m, so the area is only mean-
ingful to about one decimal place: 1.4 m². Extra digits are not extra ac-
curacy; they are a claim you cannot support.
Choosing a sensible level of accuracy
– Money is usually reported to the nearest 0.01, because that is the
smallest unit in use.
– Populations and attendance are usually reported to the nearest ten,
hundred or thousand.
– Measurements should be reported to the precision of the instru-
ment, not beyond it.
– Intermediate calculations should keep more digits than the final
answer, so that rounding errors do not accumulate.

What this chapter established
– Numbers belong to nested families; each extension of the family makes a new kind of question answer-
able.
– In base ten, a digit's value is the digit multiplied by a power of ten determined by its position.
– Zero holds a column open and is therefore essential, not decorative.
– Expanded form is an equivalent representation that exposes structure.
– Rounding replaces a value with a nearby value at a stated precision, and the choice of precision is a math-
ematical decision, not a presentation one.
Vocabulary
Term Meaning
natural number A counting number: 1, 2, 3, …
whole number A natural number or zero.
integer A whole number or its negative.
rational number A number that can be written as a fraction of two integers, with a non-zero denominator.
place value The value a digit carries because of its position.
expanded form A number written as the sum of its column values.
rounding Replacing a number with a nearby value at a stated precision.
estimate A deliberately simplified calculation used to judge size or reasonableness.
Misconceptions to watch
Three that survive into later years
Longer decimal, larger number. 0.65 is not larger than 0.7. Align columns and compare like with like.
Zero is nothing. A zero in the middle of a number does essential work; deleting it changes the value.
More digits, more accuracy. Digits beyond the precision of the measurement are a claim, not a fact.
