### 05 · EXPLAIN

*Numerical* data are numbers; they are *discrete* if the values are counted and *continuous* if they are measured. *Categorical* data are names or labels. A *population* is the whole group of interest and a *sample* is the part measured; a sample is *biased* if it does not represent the population.

**Four rules that prevent most errors**

**Good data collection**
• Ask one unambiguous question.
• Use a consistent unit and record it.
• Include every value, including zeros and unusually large ones.
• State the size of the sample and how it was chosen.

**Worked example 11.1.1 — Classifying variables**

Classify each variable: (a) shoe size, (b) time to run 100 m, (c) favourite colour, (d) number of siblings.

1. (a) Shoe size is numerical and discrete: sizes are counted in fixed steps.
2. (b) Time is numerical and continuous: it can take any value, limited only by the stopwatch.
3. (c) Favourite colour is categorical: the values are names.
4. (d) Number of siblings is numerical and discrete.

*Answer:* (a) discrete (b) continuous (c) categorical (d) discrete

*Check:* Ask whether the values are names, counts or measurements.

**Worked example 11.1.2 — Recognising a biased sample**

A survey asks students in the football club how often they exercise. Explain why the results will be biased.

1. The sample contains only students who already choose to play football.
2. They are likely to exercise more than the school population as a whole.
3. The mean from this sample will overestimate the school's exercise frequency.
4. A better sample would be chosen at random across all year groups.

*Answer:* The sample is not representative

*Check:* Bias comes from how the sample was chosen, not from the size of the numbers.

“More data is always better data.” A large biased sample is still biased — a million responses from a football club tell you about the football club. Size reduces random variation; it does nothing for bias.

## 11.2 Averages and range

**Criterion A · II · C · III** · Related concepts: Patterns, Measurement

> An average is a single number standing in for a set. There are three of them, and they disagree in useful ways.

[Photograph: ch11_screen.jpg — A spreadsheet of readings and its summary charts. The summary is a choice, and a different choice gives a different story.]

[Figure: mean_balance — The mean as a balance point: the distances of the values above it counter the distances below.]

**Mean** = sum of values ÷ number of values.
**Median** = the middle value of an ordered list; with an even count, the mean of the two middle values.
**Mode** = the most frequent value (there may be none or several).
**Range** = largest − smallest.

**Worked example 11.2.1 — All four summaries**

Find the mean, median, mode and range of 4, 7, 7, 9, 13.

1. Sum = 40 and there are 5 values, so the mean is 40 ÷ 5 = 8.
2. The ordered list has middle value 7, so the median is 7.
3. 7 occurs twice, more than any other, so the mode is 7.
4. Range = 13 − 4 = 9.
5. Check the mean by balance: 4 and 13 are 4 and 5 away from 8; 7, 7, 9 are 1 each side; the total below equals the total above.

*Answer:* mean 8, median 7, mode 7, range 9

*Check:* Order the list before finding the median, and count the values carefully.

**Worked example 11.2.2 — An even number of values**

Find the median of 3, 5, 8, 10, 12, 15.

1. There are six values, so there is no single middle value.
2. The two middle values are the 3rd and 4th: 8 and 10.
3. The median is their mean: (8 + 10) ÷ 2 = 9.
4. Check that three values lie below 9 and three above.

*Answer:* 9

*Check:* With an even count the median may not be a value in the list at all.

**Worked example 11.2.3 — Mean from a frequency table**

Find the mean of the data in the table.

1. Multiply each value by its frequency: 1×4 = 4, 2×6 = 12, 3×9 = 27, 4×5 = 20, 5×1 = 5.
2. Σ*fx* = 68 and Σ*f* = 25.
3. Mean = 68 ÷ 25 = 2.72.
4. Check the median instead: the 13th value lies in the group with value 3, so the median is 3.

*Answer:* 2.72

*Check:* Divide by the total frequency, not by the number of rows.

“The mean of 2, 3, 3, 4 and 48 is about 4, so the typist's speed is about 4.” The mean is 12, dragged up by the value 48. The median, 3, describes the typical value far better. An extreme value is an *outlier*, and it affects the mean much more than the median.

## 11.3 Displaying data

**Criterion C · II · A · II** · Related concepts: Patterns, Representation

> A display is chosen to make a pattern visible. The wrong display can hide the pattern the data actually contains.

[Figure: bar_frequency — A bar chart for the scores on a die. The bars are separated because the scores are discrete categories.]

[Figure: dot_plot — A dot plot of a small data set. Each dot is one value, so both the centre and the spread can be read at once.]

[Figure: frequency_table — A frequency table. It is the first step towards every display: the counts must be correct before anything is drawn.]

| Display | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Bar chart | comparing counts in categories | bars must have equal width and be separated |
| Dot plot | small numerical sets; spotting clusters | each dot must represent exactly one value |
| Histogram | continuous data in classes | bars touch, because the classes are continuous |
| Box plot | comparing median and spread of two sets | it hides the shape of the data |

**Worked example 11.3.1 — From table to bar chart**

The table gives the number of students choosing each subject. Describe the chart you would draw and the scale you would use.

1. The variable is categorical, so a bar chart with separated bars is appropriate.
2. The frequencies run from 6 to 18, so a vertical scale of 0 to 20 in steps of 2 fits comfortably.
3. Bars should be of equal width, with the subject names on the horizontal axis.
4. The axis must start at zero, otherwise the comparison of lengths is misleading.

*Answer:* A bar chart, 0–20 in steps of 2

*Check:* Starting a bar axis above zero exaggerates differences.

**Worked example 11.3.2 — Reading a dot plot**

A dot plot shows 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 6, 6, 7, 7, 7, 8, 9. Find the median and describe the shape.

1. There are 13 values, so the median is the 7th value.
2. Counting the dots: 3, then two 4s (3rd), three 5s (6th), so the 7th is the first 6.
3. The median is 6.
4. The dots are clustered around 5–7 with single values at 3 and 9, so the distribution is roughly symmetric with a small spread.

*Answer:* median 6; clustered around 5–7

*Check:* Count the dots cumulatively; do not average the distinct values.

**Worked example 11.3.3 — Choosing a display**

A student wants to show how the mass of 60 apples is distributed. Which display should be used, and why?

1. Mass is continuous, so the values should be grouped into equal class intervals.
2. A histogram is appropriate: the bars touch because neighbouring classes are continuous.
3. A bar chart with gaps would wrongly suggest the classes are separate categories.
4. The vertical axis shows frequency, so 0 must be included.

*Answer:* A histogram with equal class intervals

*Check:* Ask whether the variable is continuous before drawing bars.

“I have used two different colours on a bar chart, so the bars need not have equal width.” Unequal bar widths distort the comparison: readers compare area, not height. Keep bars equal in width and the gaps equal too.

## 11.4 Quartiles, spread and comparing groups

**Criterion A · III · D · IV** · Related concepts: Patterns, Measurement

> Two groups can share a mean and differ completely. Comparing spread as well as centre is what makes a comparison honest.

[Figure: box_plot_pair — Two box plots compared. The median marks the centre; the box holds the middle half of the data and the whiskers reach the extremes.]

The *lower quartile* (LQ) is the median of the lower half of the data and the *upper quartile* (UQ) the median of the upper half. The *interquartile range* (IQR) = UQ − LQ and measures the spread of the middle half. A *box plot* shows the smallest value, LQ, median, UQ and largest value.

**Worked example 11.4.1 — Quartiles and IQR**

Find the median, lower quartile, upper quartile and interquartile range of 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 11, 12, 14, 15.

1. There are 9 values, so the median is the 5th value: 9.
2. The lower half is 3, 5, 6, 8, whose median is (5 + 6) ÷ 2 = 5.5.
3. The upper half is 11, 12, 14, 15, whose median is (12 + 14) ÷ 2 = 13.
4. IQR = 13 − 5.5 = 7.5.

*Answer:* median 9, LQ 5.5, UQ 13, IQR 7.5

*Check:* Do not include the median value itself in either half when the count is odd.

**Worked example 11.4.2 — Comparing two classes**

Class A has median 62 and IQR 8; class B has median 66 and IQR 22. Compare the two classes.

1. Class B's median is higher, so its typical score is better.
2. Class A's IQR is much smaller, so its scores are tightly grouped.
3. Class B's scores are far more spread out: some are well above and some well below its median.
4. A fair statement: B is better on average, but less consistent.

*Answer:* B higher median; A far more consistent

*Check:* Compare a measure of centre and a measure of spread; one alone is never enough.

**Worked example 11.4.3 — Outliers and the IQR**

The data are 12, 14, 15, 16, 18, 19, 21, 45. Find the IQR and state whether 45 is an outlier by the 1.5 × IQR rule.

1. Median = (16 + 18) ÷ 2 = 17.
2. Lower half 12, 14, 15, 16 has LQ = 14.5; upper half 18, 19, 21, 45 has UQ = 20.
3. IQR = 20 − 14.5 = 5.5, so 1.5 × IQR = 8.25.
4. The upper boundary is 20 + 8.25 = 28.25, and 45 is above it, so 45 is an outlier.

*Answer:* IQR 5.5; 45 is an outlier

*Check:* An outlier should be investigated, not deleted: it may be a mistake or the most important value.

“Both groups have the same mean, so they are the same.” 10, 10, 10, 10 and 0, 5, 15, 20 both have mean 10, but one has no spread at all. Always report a measure of spread alongside the average.

## 11.6 Summary, revision and reflection

### What this chapter established

- Data are numerical (discrete or continuous) or categorical, and the choice determines the display.
- Mean, median and mode each describe a different sense of ‘typical’; the range and IQR describe spread.
- An outlier moves the mean much more than the median, so the median is safer for skewed data.
- The mean from a frequency table is Σ*fx* ÷ Σ*f*.
- Bar charts compare categories and histograms show continuous classes; axes must start at zero.
- Comparing two groups needs a measure of centre and a measure of spread.

**Vocabulary to use precisely**

**population** · **sample** · **bias** · **discrete** · **continuous** · **categorical** · **mean** · **median** · **mode** · **range** · **quartile** · **interquartile range** · **outlier** · **box plot**

### Misconceptions to close

| The mistake | What is really happening |
|---|---|
| Using the mean for any data set. | One extreme value can distort it; the median is better for skewed data. |
| Drawing a bar chart for continuous data. | Continuous classes need a histogram, whose bars touch. |
| Starting a bar chart axis at the smallest value. | It exaggerates differences; the axis must start at zero. |
| Comparing two groups by their means only. | Two sets can share a mean and have completely different spreads. |
