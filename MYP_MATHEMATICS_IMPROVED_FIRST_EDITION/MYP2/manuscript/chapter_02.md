Two quantities are proportional when one is always a fixed

### 01 · MYP UNIT PLANNER

*What makes a relationship proportional, and what does the constant of proportionality let you predict?*

**Statement of inquiry.** A single constant of proportionality links a model to the reality it represents, so a measurement in one can be converted into a claim about the other.

**Key and related concepts**

**Key concept · Relationships.** In a proportional relationship every pair of values has the same quotient, and the graph is a straight line through the origin.
**Related · Models.** A scale drawing or a recipe is a model: it keeps one relationship and discards everything else.

## 2.1 Recognising proportional relationships

**Criterion A · I · B · II** · Related concepts: Relationships, Constant of proportionality

> A proportional relationship is not simply a relationship that grows. It is a relationship in which every value of the second quantity is the same multiple of the first.

### 03 · ENGAGE

*Two shops, two price lists. Which shop simply charges more per rule, and which one behaves differently?*

Shop A sells ribbon at £1.20 per metre: 2 m cost £2.40, 5 m cost £6.00. Shop B charges £2 for the first metre and £1.20 for each metre after that: 2 m cost £3.20, 5 m cost £6.80. Both price lists rise. Only one of them is proportional.

[Photograph: ch02_model.jpg — An architectural scale model keeps the proportions of the building and discards its materials, its weight and its plumbing. A model is a set of deliberate decisions about what to keep.]

Before reading on, work out the cost per metre for both shops at 2 m and at 5 m. Write a sentence that says which shop's price per metre stays constant.

### 05 · EXPLAIN

Two quantities are in *direct proportion* when one is a constant multiple of the other: *y* = *k x*, where *k* is not zero. The constant *k* is the *constant of proportionality*. A relationship is proportional exactly when *y* ÷ *x* has the same value for every pair, and its graph is a straight line through the origin.

[Figure: prop_graph — A proportional relationship graphed: cost against volume at £3 per litre. Equal quotients produce equal gradients, and a zero volume costs zero.]

Two tests, used together. **Divide:** if *y* ÷ *x* is constant, the relationship is proportional. **Graph:** if the points lie on a straight line through the origin, it is proportional. A straight line that misses the origin is called linear, not proportional.

Proportional relationships are everywhere in measurement: cost and quantity at a fixed unit price; distance and time at a steady speed; the circumference and diameter of any circle; the mass and volume of one material. Recognising one means you can predict a value you have not measured.

**Worked example 2.1.1 — Find the constant**

Four litres of paint cost £34. Find the constant of proportionality and the cost of 7 litres.

1. The constant is the cost per litre: *k* = 34 ÷ 4 = 8.5.
2. Write the relationship: cost = 8.5 × litres.
3. For 7 litres: 8.5 × 7 = £59.50.
4. State the constant in words, with its unit: £8.50 per litre.

*Answer:* *k* = 8.5; 7 litres cost £59.50

*Check:* Check by ratio: 34 : 4 = 59.5 : 7, since 34 × 7 = 4 × 59.5 = 238.

**Worked example 2.1.2 — Proportional or not?**

A taxi charges £4 to start plus £2 per kilometre. Is the cost proportional to the distance? Justify with a table.

1. Build a table: 1 km £6, 2 km £8, 3 km £10, 4 km £12.
2. Divide: 6 ÷ 1 = 6, 8 ÷ 2 = 4, 10 ÷ 3 ≈ 3.33. The quotients are not constant.
3. The graph is a straight line, but it starts at £4, not at £0, so it does not pass through the origin.
4. The cost is linear but not proportional; the fixed charge is what breaks the proportionality.

*Answer:* Not proportional — the quotients differ and the line misses the origin.

*Check:* At 0 km the cost is £4, whereas a proportional relationship must give 0.

**Worked example 2.1.3 — Reasoning from the graph**

A line passes through (0, 0) and (6, 15). State the constant of proportionality and the value of *y* when *x* = 10.

1. Because the line passes through the origin, *k* = *y* ÷ *x*.
2. Using the point (6, 15): *k* = 15 ÷ 6 = 2.5.
3. Then *y* = 2.5*x*, so when *x* = 10, *y* = 25.
4. Check the point is on the line: 25 ÷ 10 = 2.5, the same constant.

*Answer:* *k* = 2.5; *y* = 25

*Check:* Any two points on a proportional line give the same quotient.

“The table adds 3 each time, so it is proportional.” Constant addition gives a linear pattern, not a proportional one: 2, 5, 8, 11 grows by 3 but 5 ÷ 2 ≠ 8 ÷ 5. Proportionality is about a constant multiplier, so test by dividing.

## 2.2 Solving proportional problems

**Criterion A · II · C · II** · Related concepts: Equivalence, Proportion

> A proportion is an equation saying that two ratios are equal. Solving one means finding the missing term without abandoning the relationship.

A *proportion* is a statement that two ratios are equal, such as *a* : *b* = *c* : *d*. The cross-product condition *a d* = *b c* follows from multiplying both sides by *b d*, and it holds for every true proportion.

[Figure: proportion_table — Scaling a proportion through a unit: multiplying the first column by 2.5 gives the fourth, and every pair keeps the quotient 3.]

### Three reliable methods

1. **Scale by a factor.** If the known pair is multiplied by *k*, multiply the other term by the same *k*.
2. **Find the unit rate.** Divide to get the value for one unit, then multiply.
3. **Cross-multiply.** Write *a*/ *b* = *c*/ *d* and solve *a d* = *b c*.

All three give the same answer when the relationship is proportional. Choose the one that keeps the numbers easiest to handle, and say which you used.

**Worked example 2.2.1 — Solve by scaling**

A recipe for 12 people needs 600 g of rice. How much rice is needed for 45 people?

1. Find the scale factor from 12 to 45: 45 ÷ 12 = 3.75.
2. Multiply the quantity by the same factor: 600 × 3.75 = 2 250 g.
3. State the answer in a sensible unit: 2.25 kg.
4. Check with the unit rate: 600 ÷ 12 = 50 g per person, and 50 × 45 = 2 250 g.

*Answer:* 2 250 g, or 2.25 kg

*Check:* Both methods agree, and 2.25 kg is plausible for 45 servings.

[Figure: proportional_recipe — Scaling a recipe by 3.75. Every ingredient is multiplied by the same factor, so the dish tastes the same.]

**Worked example 2.2.2 — Solve by cross-multiplying**

If 5 pens cost £4, how much do 8 pens cost?

1. Write the proportion: 5/ 4 = 8/ *x*, where *x* is the cost in pounds.
2. Cross-multiply: 5*x* = 32.
3. Divide both sides by 5: *x* = 6.4.
4. State the answer with its unit: £6.40.

*Answer:* 8 pens cost £6.40

*Check:* The unit price is £0.80, and 8 × 0.80 = 6.40.

**Worked example 2.2.3 — A proportion with a fractional term**

A tap fills 3/4 of a tank in 18 minutes. How long does it take to fill the whole tank?

1. Let *t* be the time for the whole tank. Then 18/ (3/4) = *t*/ 1.
2. 18 ÷ (3/4) = 18 × 4/3 = 24.
3. So *t* = 24 minutes.
4. Check: three quarters of 24 minutes is 18 minutes, as stated.

*Answer:* 24 minutes

*Check:* The whole is larger than three quarters, so the time must be larger than 18 minutes.

Setting up the proportion upside down. “5 pens cost £4, so 8 pens cost *x*” gives 5/ 8 = 4/ *x*, which solves to £6.40 only by accident here. Keep the same quantity on top of both fractions: pens over pens, money over money.

## 2.3 Scale drawings, maps and similar figures

**Criterion C · III · D · II** · Related concepts: Models, Scale

> A scale drawing is a proportional model of something too large or too small to handle at full size. Every length is multiplied by the same scale factor, and every angle is unchanged.

A drawing is to *scale* when every length in the drawing is the same multiple of the corresponding real length. The multiplier is the *scale factor*; a map is often written as a ratio, 1 : 50 000, meaning 1 unit on the map represents 50 000 of the same units on the ground.

[Figure: scale_pair — A model at 1 : 15 and the object it represents. Lengths are multiplied by 15; angles are unchanged.]

Converting between drawing and reality is a multiplication, so the only real care needed is with units. A scale of 1 : 50 000 used with centimetres gives centimetres on the ground, which must then be converted to metres or kilometres.

[Photograph: ch02_map.jpg — A compass on a printed map. The map keeps direction and relative distance, and discards height, weather and time — a model is defined as much by what it leaves out as by what it keeps.]

**Worked example 2.3.1 — Map to ground**

A map has scale 1 : 50 000. Two villages are 7 cm apart on the map. Find the real distance in kilometres.

1. Multiply the map length by the scale: 7 × 50 000 = 350 000 cm.
2. Convert to metres: 350 000 ÷ 100 = 3 500 m.
3. Convert to kilometres: 3 500 ÷ 1 000 = 3.5 km.
4. Write the answer with the unit the question asked for: 3.5 km.

*Answer:* 3.5 km

*Check:* Sanity check: 1 cm represents 0.5 km, so 7 cm represents 3.5 km.

**Worked example 2.3.2 — Ground to map**

A garden is 24 m long. On a plan it is drawn 16 cm long. Find the scale of the plan in the form 1 : *n*.

1. Convert to the same unit: 24 m = 2 400 cm.
2. The scale factor is 2 400 ÷ 16 = 150.
3. So 1 cm on the plan represents 150 cm in the garden.
4. The scale is 1 : 150.

*Answer:* 1 : 150

*Check:* Check: 16 × 150 = 2 400 cm = 24 m, as stated.

**Worked example 2.3.3 — Similar figures**

Two rectangles are similar. The first is 6 cm by 9 cm; the second has a shorter side of 10 cm. Find its longer side.

1. Similar means a single scale factor applies to every side.
2. The factor is 10 ÷ 6 = 5/3.
3. The longer side is 9 × 5/3 = 15 cm.
4. Check that the ratios of corresponding sides agree: 6 : 9 = 10 : 15, since 6 × 15 = 90 = 9 × 10.

*Answer:* 15 cm

*Check:* Cross-products agree, so the sides are in the same ratio.

“The map is 1 : 50 000, so 7 cm becomes 350 000 km.” The scale relates like units to like units: 7 cm becomes 350 000 cm, which is 3.5 km. Convert the unit before you convert the magnitude.

## 2.5 Summary, revision and reflection

### What this chapter established

- A relationship is proportional when *y* ÷ *x* is the same for every pair; its graph is a straight line through the origin.
- The constant of proportionality *k* carries units and meaning: cost per litre, kilometres per hour, centimetres per centimetre.
- A linear relationship with a non-zero starting value is not proportional.
- Proportions are solved by scaling, by unit rate, or by cross-multiplying, and all three must give the same answer.
- A scale drawing multiplies every length by the same factor and leaves every angle unchanged.
- Similar figures have equal angles and proportional sides.

**Vocabulary to use precisely**

**proportion** · **proportional** · **constant of proportionality** · **unit rate** · **scale** · **scale factor** · **scale drawing** · **similar** · **cross-multiply** · **linear (not proportional)**

### Misconceptions to close

| The mistake | What is really happening |
|---|---|
| “It grows by the same amount, so it is proportional.” | Constant addition is linear, not proportional. 2, 5, 8 has a constant difference of 3 but quotients 2.5, 2.5, 2.67 that do not agree. |
| “Any straight line is proportional.” | Only a line through the origin is. A £4 call-out charge breaks proportionality. |
| “Scale 1 : 50 000 means the map is 50 000 times smaller in every way.” | It is 50 000 times smaller in length; areas scale by the square of the factor. |
| “Similar shapes have equal sides.” | Congruent shapes have equal sides; similar shapes have proportional sides. |
