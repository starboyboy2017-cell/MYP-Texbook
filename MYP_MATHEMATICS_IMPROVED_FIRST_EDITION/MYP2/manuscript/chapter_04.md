# Chapter 4 · Integers and operations

*Part II · Algebra and pattern* · key concept **Logic** · related concepts: Quantity, Change

Global context: Scientific and technical innovation

Negative numbers extend the number line in both directions.
Their arithmetic is not a set of rules to memorise but a
consequence of one idea: every number has an opposite, and
opposites cancel.

### 01 · MYP UNIT PLANNER

*Signed numbers on the line, and the arithmetic that keeps the line consistent.*

**Statement of inquiry.** Extending the number line below zero forces a consistent arithmetic of signs, so that every calculation can be represented as movement along a line.

**Key and related concepts**

**Key concept · Logic.** The rules for signed arithmetic are not arbitrary: each follows from requiring that the number line and the laws of arithmetic keep working below zero.
**Related · Quantity.** A negative number records a quantity in the opposite direction: a debt, a fall, a depth.
**Related · Change.** Adding a negative number is a move left; subtracting a negative is a move right.

**Global context · Scientific and technical innovation**

Exploration: temperatures below freezing, depths below sea level and balances below zero. Instruments report signed quantities because the world has directions, not only sizes.

### Inquiry questions

- **Factual:** How do you order and compare negative numbers?
- **Conceptual:** Why is subtracting a negative the same as adding a positive?
- **Debatable:** Does a negative number measure anything real, or is it only a bookkeeping device?

## 4.1 Integers, order and absolute value

**Criterion A · I** · Related concepts: Quantity, Number line

> An integer is a whole number that may be positive, negative or zero. The number line shows all of them, equally spaced, with zero in the middle.

### 03 · ENGAGE

*Two readings, one morning. Which is colder, and by how much?*

[Photograph: ch04_thermometer.jpg — A thermometer in snow. The scale continues below zero, which is exactly what the integers were invented to describe.]

### 05 · EXPLAIN

[Photograph: ch04_stairs.jpg — A flight of steps. Each step is the same height, so the treads give a natural number line: up is positive, down is negative.]

An *integer* is a whole number that may be positive, negative or zero. The *absolute value* of an integer is its distance from zero, written with vertical bars: |−5| = 5 and |5| = 5. On the number line, larger means further right, so −2 > −5.

[Figure: signed_line — Integers ordered on the number line. The arrow shows the change from −3 to 2: five steps to the right, so the change is +5.]

Adding a negative number moves left; subtracting a positive number also moves left. Subtracting a negative number moves right, because removing a debt increases what you have. The number line makes both statements visible, which is why it is worth drawing before computing.

|−*a*| = |*a*|: a number and its opposite are the same distance from zero. That is why cancelling one +1 with one −1 leaves nothing: the two distances are equal and opposite.

[Figure: integer_chips — Three +1 chips and five −1 chips. Each opposite pair cancels, leaving two −1 chips: 3 + (−5) = −2.]

**Worked example 4.1.1 — Order and absolute value**

Order −4, 3, −7, 0, −1 from least to greatest, then write the absolute value of each.

1. On the number line, further left is smaller: −7 < −4 < −1 < 0 < 3.
2. Take distances from zero: |−7| = 7, |−4| = 4, |−1| = 1, |0| = 0, |3| = 3.
3. Note that the order of the numbers and the order of their absolute values differ.
4. Report both orders, since the question asks for both.

*Answer:* −7 < −4 < −1 < 0 < 3; absolute values 7, 4, 1, 0, 3

*Check:* The smallest number has the largest absolute value — a useful check.

**Worked example 4.1.2 — Two readings, one change**

A temperature rises from −9°C to −2°C. What is the change?

1. Change = final − initial = −2 − (−9).
2. Subtracting −9 is the same as adding 9: −2 + 9 = 7.
3. The change is +7 degrees, that is, a rise of 7 degrees.
4. Check on the line: from −9, move right 7 places to −2.

*Answer:* A rise of 7 degrees

*Check:* A negative answer would have meant a fall; the sign carries the direction.

“−9 is bigger than −2, so the temperature rose by 11.” The sign of the answer comes from the calculation −2 − (−9) = +7, not from the sizes of the digits. Compare positions on the line, not digit values.

## 4.2 Adding and subtracting integers

**Criterion A · II** · Related concepts: Change, Number line

> Every addition of a signed number can be read as a movement along the line, and every subtraction as a movement in the reverse direction.

### Two equivalent readings

- **Movement:** −8 + 3 means start at −8 and move 3 right, finishing at −5.
- **Cancelling:** −8 + 3 combines eight −1 chips with three +1 chips; three pairs cancel, leaving five −1 chips.

Both readings give the same answer, and both are worth knowing. The movement reading is fast for small numbers; the cancelling reading explains why the answer has the sign it does.

**Worked example 4.2.1 — Add and subtract**

Calculate (a) −5 − 7, (b) 4 − (−6), (c) −3 + (−4) + 9.

1. (a) Subtracting 7 moves 7 left from −5: the result is −12.
2. (b) Subtracting −6 is the same as adding 6, so 4 + 6 = 10.
3. (c) Combine the negatives first: −3 + (−4) = −7, then −7 + 9 = 2.
4. Check (b) on the line: from 4, move 6 right, finishing at 10.

*Answer:* (a) −12   (b) 10   (c) 2

*Check:* For (c), the total leftward movement is 7 and the rightward movement is 9, so the net movement is 2 right.

**Worked example 4.2.2 — A real balance**

An account balance is −£120. A payment of £50 is made into it, then a bill of £80 is taken out. Find the balance and interpret it.

1. Start at −120.
2. Adding £50: −120 + 50 = −70.
3. Subtracting £80: −70 − 80 = −150.
4. The balance is −£150: the account is overdrawn by £150.

*Answer:* −£150, an overdraft of £150

*Check:* Check the net change: +50 − 80 = −30, and −120 − 30 = −150.

**Worked example 4.2.3 — A distance between two points**

The temperature at the summit is −8°C and at the base −3°C. How much colder is the summit?

1. Change = summit − base = −8 − (−3).
2. −8 + 3 = −5.
3. A change of −5 degrees means the summit is 5 degrees colder.
4. Check on the line: −8 is five places left of −3.

*Answer:* 5 degrees colder

*Check:* The sign gives the direction; the size gives the amount.

“4 − (−6) = −2, because the two minus signs cancel.” Cancelling does happen, but it produces addition: 4 − (−6) = 4 + 6 = 10. Two signs together are not a multiplication.

## 4.3 Multiplying and dividing integers

**Criterion A · III · B · III** · Related concepts: Logic, Change

> Multiplication of signs follows from one requirement: the number line and the laws of arithmetic must keep working when negative numbers are included.

### Repeated addition

3 × (−4) means three lots of −4: −4 + (−4) + (−4) = −12. When the first factor is negative, the reading is less obvious, and the pattern below settles it.

[Figure: neg_mult — The product −2 × *n* as *n* falls by 1. Each step raises the product by 2, and continuing past zero forces −2 × (−1) = +2.]

The four cases, in one line: (+) × (+) = +, (+) × (−) = −, (−) × (+) = −, (−) × (−) = +. Exactly two negatives give a positive; one negative gives a negative.

**Worked example 4.3.1 — Compute with signs**

Calculate (a) −6 × 4, (b) −6 × (−4), (c) −48 ÷ 8, (d) −48 ÷ (−6).

1. (a) One negative factor, so the product is negative: −24.
2. (b) Two negative factors, so the product is positive: 24.
3. (c) One negative term, so the quotient is negative: −6.
4. (d) Two negatives, so the quotient is positive: 8.

*Answer:* (a) −24   (b) 24   (c) −6   (d) 8

*Check:* Check (d) by multiplying back: 8 × (−6) = −48.

**Worked example 4.3.2 — Order of operations with signs**

Evaluate −3² + 4 × (−2) − (−5).

1. Indices first, and the minus sign is not part of the square: −3² = −9.
2. Multiplication next: 4 × (−2) = −8.
3. Now the expression reads −9 − 8 + 5.
4. Working left to right: −17 + 5 = −12.

*Answer:* −12

*Check:* Note that (−3)² = +9: the brackets matter, and so does the order.

**Worked example 4.3.3 — Division as the inverse**

Given that −7 × 9 = −63, write two division statements and explain the signs.

1. Divide the product by one factor: −63 ÷ 9 = −7.
2. Divide by the other factor: −63 ÷ (−7) = 9.
3. Each quotient has exactly one negative because each product has exactly one negative factor.
4. The two statements check each other: they reconstruct the original product.

*Answer:* −63 ÷ 9 = −7 and −63 ÷ (−7) = 9

*Check:* Division is multiplication read backwards, so the sign rules must agree.

Writing −3² = 9. Without brackets, the square applies only to 3, so −3² = −(3 × 3) = −9. With brackets, (−3)² = +9. Write the brackets whenever the sign is inside the power.

### 06 · INVESTIGATE AND APPLY

*Why is a negative times a negative positive?*

### Why is negative times negative positive?  (Investigation)

The rule (−) × (−) = + is often given as something to memorise. This investigation derives it, and tests what would happen if it were different.

1. Complete the table of −3 × *n* for *n* = 4, 3, 2, 1, 0, −1, −2. What happens to the product each time *n* falls by 1?
2. Continue the pattern for another three rows. Does the pattern force a particular value at each step?
3. Now suppose (−3) × (−1) were −3 instead of +3. Complete the table again with that assumption and describe what happens to the step size.
4. Test the same reasoning starting from −5 × *n*. Does the conclusion depend on the choice of −3 or −5?
5. Use a different argument: the distributive law gives (−2) × (3 + (−3)) = 0. Expand and see what value (−2) × (−3) must have.
6. Write your generalisation in one sentence, and name the two principles it relies on.
7. Find one place in real life where a negative times negative naturally appears, and explain it.

## 4.6 Summary, revision and reflection

### What this chapter established

- Integers continue the number line below zero; larger means further right, not further from zero.
- The absolute value is the distance from zero, and it is never negative.
- Adding a negative moves left; subtracting a negative moves right.
- The sign of a product or quotient depends only on the number of negative factors: an even count gives positive, an odd count gives negative.
- The order of operations applies to signed numbers, and brackets around a sign change the meaning of a power.
- Signed numbers model debt, depth, temperature and any quantity with a direction.

**Vocabulary to use precisely**

**integer** · **negative number** · **opposite** · **absolute value** · **number line** · **sign** · **order of operations** · **index (power)** · **inverse operation**

### Misconceptions to close

| The mistake | What is really happening |
|---|---|
| “−9 is larger than −2 because 9 > 2.” | −9 is further left, so it is smaller. The digits describe the distance, the sign describes the direction. |
| “Two minus signs always cancel by disappearing.” | They combine into a plus: 4 − (−6) = 4 + 6 = 10. |
| “−3² = 9.” | Without brackets the square applies to 3 only: −3² = −9, while (−3)² = 9. |
| “A negative answer means the calculation is wrong.” | A negative answer means a direction or a deficit; it can be entirely correct. |
