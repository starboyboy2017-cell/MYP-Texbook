# Chapter 8 · 3D solids, surface area and volume

*Part III · Geometry and space* · key concept **Form** · related concepts: Space, Measurement

Global context: Globalisation and sustainability

A solid is a surface you could wrap and a space you could fill.
This chapter separates those two questions — how much
material, and how much room — because they are answered
by different formulas.

### 01 · MYP UNIT PLANNER

*From describing a solid to measuring its skin and its interior.*

**Statement of inquiry.** The form of a three-dimensional object determines both the material needed to make it and the space it can hold, so design is a negotiation between surface and volume.

**Related · Measurement.** Both quantities are computed from a few lengths, with units that must be tracked carefully.

### 05 · EXPLAIN

A *face* is a flat surface, an *edge* is where two faces meet, and a *vertex* is a corner where edges meet. A *net* is a flat pattern that folds to make the solid. For any convex polyhedron, *V* − *E* + *F* = 2 (Euler's relationship).

### Naming solids

| Solid | Defining property | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Prism | uniform cross-section, flat ends | cuboid, triangular prism |
| Cylinder | uniform circular cross-section | tin, pipe, tank |
| Pyramid | flat base, triangular faces meeting at an apex | Egyptian pyramid |
| Cone | circular base, curved surface to an apex | ice-cream cone |
| Sphere | every point the same distance from the centre | ball, globe |

**Worked example 8.1.1 — Using Euler's relationship**

A solid has 12 faces and 30 edges. How many vertices does it have?

1. Euler's relationship is *V* − *E* + *F* = 2.
2. Substitute: *V* − 30 + 12 = 2.
3. So *V* − 18 = 2 and *V* = 20.
4. Check: 20 − 30 + 12 = 2, as required.

*Answer:* 20 vertices

*Check:* Substitute before rearranging; the arithmetic is less error-prone.

**Worked example 8.1.2 — Counting a prism**

A prism has a hexagonal cross-section. Find its number of faces, edges and vertices, and check Euler's relationship.

1. A hexagon has 6 sides, so the ends are 2 faces and the sides are 6 rectangles: *F* = 8.
2. Count the edges in three groups: 6 round the top, 6 round the bottom and 6 vertical, so *E* = 18.
3. Each end has 6 vertices: *V* = 12.
4. Check: 12 − 18 + 8 = 2, as Euler requires.

*Answer:* *F* = 8, *E* = 18, *V* = 12

*Check:* Counting in a system — all top edges, then all bottom, then all vertical — prevents double counting.

“A cylinder has 2 faces, 0 edges and 0 vertices, so Euler's relationship fails.” Euler's relationship applies to polyhedra, solids with flat faces only. A cylinder's curved surface is not a face in this sense, so the relationship is simply not being claimed here.

## 8.2 Surface area

**Criterion A · II · D · II** · Related concepts: Measurement, Models

> Surface area is the total area of the outside of a solid: the material you would need to make it. A net makes the calculation visible.

[Figure: prism_net — The net of a triangular prism. Each face appears exactly once, so the surface area is the sum of five flat areas.]

**Prism or cuboid:** surface area = sum of the areas of all faces.
**Cylinder (closed):** *S* = 2π*r*² + 2π*r**h* — two circles plus the curved rectangle.
**Cylinder (open top):** omit one circle, *S* = π*r*² + 2π*r**h*.

[Figure: cylinder_net — The curved surface of a cylinder unrolls into a rectangle of width 2πr and height h, which is why the curved area is 2πrh.]

**Worked example 8.2.1 — A cuboid**

Find the surface area of a cuboid measuring 8 cm by 5 cm by 3 cm.

1. The three pairs of faces have areas 8×5 = 40, 8×3 = 24 and 5×3 = 15 cm².
2. Each area occurs twice: 2(40 + 24 + 15).
3. = 2 × 79 = 158 cm².
4. Check by imagining the net: six rectangles, three sizes, each repeated.

*Answer:* 158 cm²

*Check:* Adding the three different face areas first, then doubling, is faster than six separate calculations.

**Worked example 8.2.2 — A closed cylinder**

Find the surface area of a closed cylinder of radius 5 cm and height 12 cm, using π = 3.14.

1. Two circular ends: 2 × 3.14 × 5² = 157 cm².
2. Curved surface: 2π*r**h* = 2 × 3.14 × 5 × 12 = 376.8 cm².
3. Total = 157 + 376.8 = 533.8 cm².
4. Check the curved area as a rectangle 31.4 cm by 12 cm: 376.8 cm².

*Answer:* 533.8 cm²

*Check:* If the cylinder has no lid, subtract one circle — that is 78.5 cm² less.

**Worked example 8.2.3 — A triangular prism**

A triangular prism has a right-angled triangular cross-section with legs 3 cm and 4 cm, and length 10 cm. Find its surface area.

1. Cross-section area = ½ × 3 × 4 = 6 cm²; two ends give 12 cm².
2. The hypotenuse is 5 cm (3-4-5 triangle), so the perimeter of the end is 3 + 4 + 5 = 12 cm.
3. The three rectangular sides have total area 12 × 10 = 120 cm².
4. Surface area = 12 + 120 = 132 cm².

*Answer:* 132 cm²

*Check:* The perimeter of the cross-section × length gives all the rectangular faces at once.

“The surface area of a cylinder is 2π*r**h*, because that is the only curved part.” The two circular ends are also surface: omitting them loses 2π*r*², which for *r* = 5 cm is 157 cm² — nearly a third of the total.

## 8.3 Volume of prisms and cylinders

**Criterion A · III · D · III** · Related concepts: Measurement, Form

> Volume measures the space inside a solid. For any prism, one multiplication of the cross-section by the length settles it.

**Prism:** *V* = area of cross-section × length.
**Cuboid:** *V* = *l*×*w*×*h*. **Cylinder:** *V* = π*r*²*h*.
**Cone:** *V* = ⅓π*r*²*h*. **Sphere:** *V* = ⅔π*r*³.
**Capacity:** 1 litre = 1000 cm³ = 0.001 m³.

[Figure: tank_sketch — A rectangular tank partly filled. The capacity in litres is the volume in cubic centimetres divided by 1000.]

[Photograph: ch08_tank.jpg — A cylindrical storage tank. Its capacity is πr²h; its material cost is the surface area of the same cylinder.]

**Worked example 8.3.1 — Cuboid and prism**

Find the volume of (a) a cuboid 6 cm by 4 cm by 2 cm and (b) a prism whose cross-section has area 12 cm² and whose length is 7 cm.

1. (a) Volume = 6 × 4 × 2 = 48 cm³.
2. (b) Volume = cross-section × length = 12 × 7 = 84 cm³.
3. Both answers are in cubic centimetres: three lengths were multiplied.
4. Check (b) by imagining seven slices each 12 cm² thick.

*Answer:* (a) 48 cm³ (b) 84 cm³

*Check:* Slice-and-stack is the meaning of volume; the formula is just the shortcut.

**Worked example 8.3.2 — A cylinder, and a capacity**

A cylindrical tank has diameter 1 m and height 2 m. Find its volume in cubic metres and its capacity in litres, using π = 3.14.

1. Radius = 0.5 m.
2. Volume = π*r*²*h* = 3.14 × 0.25 × 2 = 1.57 m³.
3. 1 m³ = 1000 litres, so the capacity is 1570 litres.
4. Check: 0.5 m radius gives a cross-section of 0.785 m², and two metres of depth doubles it.

*Answer:* 1.57 m³ = 1570 litres

*Check:* Convert after computing; converting first multiplies the chance of error.

**Worked example 8.3.3 — Working backwards**

A cylinder of radius 5 cm has volume 314 cm³. Find its height.

1. *V* = π*r*²*h*, so 314 = 3.14 × 25 × *h*.
2. 3.14 × 25 = 78.5, so *h* = 314 ÷ 78.5 = 4.
3. The height is 4 cm.
4. Check: 78.5 × 4 = 314, as given.

*Answer:* 4 cm

*Check:* Divide by the area of the base: height = volume ÷ base area.

“A tank 1 m by 1 m by 1 m holds 100 litres.” It holds 1000 litres: 1 m³ = 100 × 100 × 100 cm³ = 1 000 000 cm³, and each litre is 1000 cm³. Volume units convert by cubes of the length conversion, not by the conversion itself.

## 8.4 Composite solids

**Criterion C · III · D · IV** · Related concepts: Form, Models

> Most manufactured objects are combinations of simple solids. Splitting them correctly decides whether the answer is right.

[Figure: composite_solid — An L-shaped solid treated as two prisms. Adding the parts works for volume; for surface area the shared faces must be removed.]

Volumes add without complication, because the parts do not overlap. Surface areas do not: where two solids are joined, the faces that touch disappear from the surface, and both must be subtracted.

**Worked example 8.4.1 — Two cuboids**

A solid is made by stacking a cuboid 8 cm by 5 cm by 3 cm on a cuboid 8 cm by 5 cm by 2 cm. Find its volume and its surface area.

1. Volumes add: 8×5×3 = 120 and 8×5×2 = 80, total 200 cm³.
2. Surface areas separately: 2(40 + 24 + 15) = 158 and 2(40 + 16 + 10) = 132, total 290 cm².
3. The joined faces are two 8 × 5 rectangles, 40 cm² each, and both disappear: subtract 80.
4. Surface area = 290 − 80 = 210 cm².

*Answer:* 200 cm³; 210 cm²

*Check:* Only the contact faces vanish, not the whole of each solid's base.

**Worked example 8.4.2 — A hole through a solid**

A cuboid 20 cm by 10 cm by 6 cm has a cylindrical hole of radius 2 cm drilled right through it. Find the volume of material remaining.

1. Cuboid volume = 20 × 10 × 6 = 1200 cm³.
2. Cylinder volume = π × 2² × 6 = 3.14 × 4 × 6 = 75.36 cm³.
3. Remaining = 1200 − 75.36 = 1124.64 cm³.
4. The hole is 6 cm long because it goes through the 6 cm thickness.

*Answer:* 1124.64 cm³

*Check:* The length of the hole equals the dimension it passes through, not the largest dimension.

**Worked example 8.4.3 — A swimming pool**

A pool is 25 m long, 10 m wide and 2 m deep, filled to 10 cm below the top. Find the volume of water in litres.

1. Water depth = 2 − 0.1 = 1.9 m.
2. Volume = 25 × 10 × 1.9 = 475 m³.
3. 1 m³ = 1000 litres, so the water is 475 000 litres.
4. Check the full capacity: 500 m³ = 500 000 litres, and 475 000 is plausibly less.

*Answer:* 475 000 litres

*Check:* Read the depth given, not the depth of the pool: the question says how full it is.

“The surface area of the stacked solid is the sum of the two surface areas, because stacking does not change either one.” Stacking hides two rectangles — one from each solid — so the total falls. Only volume is unaffected by joining.

## 8.6 Summary, revision and reflection

### What this chapter established

- Solids are described by faces, edges and vertices; *V* − *E* + *F* = 2 for polyhedra.
- A net shows every face once, so it turns surface area into a sum of flat areas.
- Surface area of a closed cylinder is 2π*r*² + 2π*r**h*; an open one loses a circle.
- Volume of any prism is cross-section × length; a cylinder is the same rule with a circular cross-section.
- 1 litre = 1000 cm³ and 1 m³ = 1000 litres.
- Volumes of joined solids add; surface areas do not, because contact faces are hidden.

**Vocabulary to use precisely**

**face** · **edge** · **vertex** · **net** · **prism** · **cross-section** · **surface area** · **volume** · **capacity** · **composite solid** · **Euler's relationship**

### Misconceptions to close

| The mistake | What is really happening |
|---|---|
| Forgetting the two circular ends of a cylinder. | They are surface too; only an open top removes one circle. |
| Using the diameter in π*r*². | Halve it first; otherwise the answer is four times too large. |
| Adding surface areas of joined solids. | Contact faces are no longer on the surface and must be subtracted twice. |
| Converting m³ to litres by multiplying by 100. | 1 m³ = 1000 litres; volume conversions use cubes of the length conversion. |
