Probability does not tell you what will happen. It measures

### 05 · EXPLAIN

The *probability* of an event A is written *P*(A). If all outcomes are equally likely, *P*(A) = (number of favourable outcomes) ÷ (total number of outcomes). For any event, 0 ≤ *P*(A) ≤ 1, and *P*(not A) = 1 − *P*(A).

| Probability | 0 | 0.25 | 0.5 | 0.75 | 1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Words | impossible | unlikely | even chance | likely | certain |
| Fraction | 0 | 1/4 | 1/2 | 3/4 | 1 |
| Percentage | 0% | 25% | 50% | 75% | 100% |

**Worked example 10.1.1 — A single event**

A bag contains 3 red and 5 blue counters. One is taken at random. Find the probability that it is (a) red, (b) blue, (c) not red.

1. There are 8 counters in total.
2. (a) *P*(red) = 3/8.
3. (b) *P*(blue) = 5/8.
4. (c) *P*(not red) = 1 − 3/8 = 5/8, the same as *P*(blue) because every counter is either red or blue.
5. Check: 3/8 + 5/8 = 1.

*Answer:* (a) 3/8 (b) 5/8 (c) 5/8

*Check:* Probabilities of all possible outcomes always total 1.

**Worked example 10.1.2 — Three forms**

A spinner has 4 equal sectors, one of which is gold. Write the probability of gold as a fraction, a decimal and a percentage.

1. Favourable outcomes 1, total 4, so *P*(gold) = 1/4.
2. 1 ÷ 4 = 0.25.
3. 0.25 = 25%.
4. The complement, *P*(not gold), is 0.75 or 75%.

*Answer:* 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%

*Check:* All three forms describe the same number; choose the form the question asks for.

**Worked example 10.1.3 — Working from a probability**

A box contains 20 counters, some red. The probability of drawing red is 0.3. How many red counters are there?

1. *P*(red) = (number red) ÷ (total) = 0.3.
2. Number red = 0.3 × 20 = 6.
3. Check: 6/20 = 0.3, as required.
4. The other 14 counters are not red.

*Answer:* 6 red counters

*Check:* Multiply the probability by the total to recover a count.

“The probability of winning is 1.2, because there are more chances of winning than losing.” A probability can never exceed 1: 1.2 would mean more favourable outcomes than outcomes. Ratios of chances are not probabilities.

## 10.2 Sample spaces

**Criterion A · II · C · III** · Related concepts: Chance, Logic

> A sample space is a complete list of what could happen. Listing it systematically turns a probability question into a counting question.

[Photograph: ch10_dice.jpg — A pair of dice. Six faces each give thirty-six ordered pairs, and the pairs are not all equally likely to give the same total.]

[Figure: sample_grid — A sample-space grid for rolling two dice. Thirty-six equally likely cells allow every sum to be counted rather than guessed.]

A *sample space* is the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment. Two events are *mutually exclusive* if they cannot happen at the same time; their probabilities then add.

**Worked example 10.2.1 — Two coins**

Two fair coins are tossed. List the sample space and find the probability of (a) two heads, (b) exactly one head.

1. The sample space is HH, HT, TH, TT — four outcomes, all equally likely.
2. (a) HH occurs once, so *P*(two heads) = 1/4.
3. (b) HT and TH give exactly one head, so *P* = 2/4 = 1/2.
4. Check: 1/4 + 1/2 + 1/4 = 1, counting also TT.

*Answer:* (a) 1/4 (b) 1/2

*Check:* HT and TH are different outcomes; treating them as one gives the classic wrong answer of 1/3.

**Worked example 10.2.2 — Two dice**

Two fair dice are rolled. Find the probability that the total is (a) 7 and (b) less than 4.

1. There are 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely outcomes.
2. (a) The total is 7 for (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1): six outcomes, so *P* = 6/36 = 1/6.
3. (b) Totals below 4 are 2 and 3: (1,1) gives 2; (1,2) and (2,1) give 3, so three outcomes.
4. *P*(less than 4) = 3/36 = 1/12.

*Answer:* (a) 1/6 (b) 1/12

*Check:* Counting from the grid prevents the common error of assuming totals are equally likely.

**Worked example 10.2.3 — A coin and a die**

A coin is tossed and a die is rolled. How many outcomes are in the sample space, and what is the probability of a head and an even number?

1. The coin has 2 outcomes and the die has 6, so there are 2 × 6 = 12 outcomes.
2. Even numbers on the die are 2, 4 and 6, three outcomes.
3. Together with a head this gives 3 favourable outcomes.
4. *P*(head and even) = 3/12 = 1/4.

*Answer:* 12 outcomes; *P* = 1/4

*Check:* Multiply the numbers of outcomes, then count the favourable ones.

“With two dice, a total of 7 and a total of 8 are equally likely because each can be made in several ways.” A 7 can be made in six ways and an 8 in five, so 7 is more likely. Only the sample-space grid shows this.

## 10.3 Tree diagrams and combined events

**Criterion A · III · C · IV** · Related concepts: Models, Logic

> A tree diagram shows a sequence of events branch by branch. Multiply along a branch for “and”; add between branches for “or”.

[Photograph: ch10_coins.jpg — Two coins. HT and TH are different outcomes, which is why counting the sample space carefully matters.]

[Figure: tree_diagram — A tree diagram for two tosses of a coin. Multiplying along a branch gives the probability of both outcomes occurring.]

**Multiplication rule:** for independent events, *P*(A and B) = *P*(A) × *P*(B).
**Addition rule:** for mutually exclusive events, *P*(A or B) = *P*(A) + *P*(B).
**Without replacement**, the second probability changes because the first item has been removed.

**Worked example 10.3.1 — Two independent events**

A bag has 4 red and 6 blue counters. One is taken, replaced, then another is taken. Find *P*(both red) and *P*(one of each colour).

1. The total stays 10 because the counter is replaced.
2. *P*(red) = 4/10 = 0.4 on each draw.
3. *P*(both red) = 0.4 × 0.4 = 0.16.
4. *P*(one of each) = *P*(RB) + *P*(BR) = 0.4×0.6 + 0.6×0.4 = 0.24 + 0.24 = 0.48.

*Answer:* *P*(RR) = 0.16; *P*(one of each) = 0.48

*Check:* There are two orders for “one of each”, so both branches must be included.

**Worked example 10.3.2 — Without replacement**

A bag has 4 red and 6 blue counters. Two are taken without replacement. Find *P*(both red) and *P*(one of each colour).

1. First draw: *P*(red) = 4/10. After removing one red, 3 red remain out of 9.
2. *P*(RR) = 4/10 × 3/9 = 12/90 = 2/15.
3. *P*(RB) = 4/10 × 6/9 = 24/90 and *P*(BR) = 6/10 × 4/9 = 24/90.
4. *P*(one of each) = 48/90 = 8/15.

*Answer:* *P*(RR) = 2/15; *P*(one of each) = 8/15

*Check:* Both the numerator and the denominator change on the second draw.

**Worked example 10.3.3 — At least one**

The probability of rain on any day is 0.3, independently. Find the probability of rain on at least one of two days.

1. Easier to find the complement: no rain on a day has probability 0.7.
2. *P*(no rain on either day) = 0.7 × 0.7 = 0.49.
3. *P*(at least one rainy day) = 1 − 0.49 = 0.51.
4. Check that this is more than 0.3 and less than 0.6, as it must be.

*Answer:* 0.51

*Check:* “At least one” is almost always quicker through the complement.

“Two events each with probability 0.5 have probability 1 of happening between them.” Adding probabilities works only for mutually exclusive events. If both can happen, adding double counts the overlap, and the total can even exceed 1.

## 10.4 Experimental probability and simulation

**Criterion B · I · D · III** · Related concepts: Chance, Models

> Experimental probability comes from data. As the number of trials grows, the relative frequency settles towards the theoretical probability — which is why a simulation can test a model.

*Experimental probability* (relative frequency) = number of times the event occurred ÷ number of trials. *Expected frequency* = probability × number of trials. A *simulation* replaces a real experiment with a random device that models it.

**Worked example 10.4.1 — From a table of results**

A spinner is spun 200 times; red appears 84 times. Find the experimental probability of red and comment on whether the spinner looks fair if red has one third of the sectors.

1. Experimental probability = 84 ÷ 200 = 0.42.
2. The theoretical probability for a fair spinner with one third red is about 0.333.
3. The experimental value is higher, but 200 trials still allow noticeable variation.
4. A conclusion needs more trials: the difference is suggestive, not conclusive.

*Answer:* 0.42; inconclusive but worth more trials

*Check:* Small samples vary; state how many trials would settle the question.

**Worked example 10.4.2 — Expected frequency**

A die is rolled 150 times. How many sixes would you expect, and how many would you expect for an even number?

1. *P*(six) = 1/6, so expected sixes = 1/6 × 150 = 25.
2. *P*(even) = 1/2, so expected even numbers = 1/2 × 150 = 75.
3. Expected values are averages over many repetitions, not predictions of a single run.
4. Check: 25 × 6 = 150, which is consistent.

*Answer:* 25 sixes; 75 even numbers

*Check:* Expectation multiplies the probability by the number of trials.

**Worked example 10.4.3 — Designing a simulation**

Design a simulation to estimate the probability that, in a group of 30 people, at least two share a birthday. State the model, the trials and the comparison.

1. Model: each person's birthday is a random whole number from 1 to 365, chosen independently.
2. Use a random-number generator to produce 30 numbers in that range; check whether any repeat.
3. Repeat for many trials — 100 or more — and count the trials with a repeat.
4. The estimate is (trials with a repeat) ÷ (total trials); compare with the theoretical value of about 0.71.

*Answer:* Random numbers 1–365, 30 per trial, many trials

*Check:* A simulation must state what is random, how it is generated, and how many trials.

“The experimental probability is 0.42, so the true probability is 0.42.” An experimental value estimates the true probability and varies from sample to sample. It becomes more reliable as the number of trials increases, but it never becomes exact.

## 10.6 Summary, revision and reflection

### What this chapter established

- Probability lies between 0 and 1, and *P*(not A) = 1 − *P*(A).
- For equally likely outcomes, *P*(A) = favourable ÷ total.
- A sample space lists every outcome; a grid makes two-stage experiments countable.
- Multiply along branches of a tree for “and”, add between branches for “or”.
- Without replacement, the second probability changes.
- Experimental probability estimates theoretical probability and improves with more trials.

**Vocabulary to use precisely**

**probability** · **outcome** · **event** · **sample space** · **equally likely** · **mutually exclusive** · **independent** · **complement** · **relative frequency** · **expected frequency** · **simulation**

### Misconceptions to close

| The mistake | What is really happening |
|---|---|
| Two coins give three outcomes, so *P*(two heads) = 1/3. | HT and TH are different, giving four equally likely outcomes and *P* = 1/4. |
| Adding probabilities of events that can happen together. | Addition is for mutually exclusive events; otherwise the overlap is counted twice. |
| Assuming a total of 7 and a total of 8 are equally likely. | There are six ways to score 7 and five ways to score 8. |
| Thinking an experimental probability is the exact probability. | It is an estimate that varies; more trials narrow the variation. |
