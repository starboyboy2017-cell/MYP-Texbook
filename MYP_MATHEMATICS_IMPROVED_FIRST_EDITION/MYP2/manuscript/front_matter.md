How to use this coursebook
This is a working book, not a book of answers to memorise. Read with a pencil in hand,
test ideas, write down conjectures, and return to your reasoning when it needs repair.
The course is arranged in four parts. Each part gathers chapters that share a way of thinking:
comparing quantities; generalising with symbols; describing space and measurement; and
making decisions from uncertainty and data. The chapters build on one another, but each is
also designed to be entered on its own after the prerequisite check.
Every chapter follows the same inquiry rhythm. You meet a situation that mathematics can clarify. You frame a question. You build the concepts and representations needed to answer it.
You practise until the method is reliable. Then you investigate, communicate, apply and reflect. The answer key at the back supports this cycle; it should confirm your reasoning, not replace it.

Statements of inquiry, and how to read them
A statement of inquiry is one sentence that names the concept, the context and the direction of
the relationship between them. It is written so that it can be tested. In this book you will find it
at the head of each chapter's planner page; by the end of the chapter you should be able to explain it in your own words and give one example that supports it.
Example, chapter 1
“Equivalent ratios preserve a multiplicative relationship, so one comparison can organise fair
shares and scaled quantities across different totals and units.” Read it as a claim with two halves:
the first says what is preserved; the second says what that preservation is good for.

Strands are named, not scored
An item is labelled with the criterion and strands it exercises — for example “Criterion C · iii, iv”.
The book does not award levels: achievement levels are a professional judgement made against
the current official criteria, using the evidence a student produces.
Support, core and extension
Each practice set is banded: the support items rehearse the method in the form it was taught;
the core items require a choice; the extension items ask for generalisation or a second representation. The banding is printed beside the practice heading so that a student can aim deliberately rather than guess.
ATL · Critical thinking
In order for a student to judge which practice items to attempt first, students must read the tag, recall what that kind of thinking demands, and choose a deliberate starting point.

Five habits that prevent most lost marks
1.
Write the unit on every quantity, including intermediate results.
2. Show the substitution before the arithmetic: “y = 3(4) + 1”, then the value.
3. Keep equals signs aligned down the page; one equals sign per line means one true statement.
4. Do not round in the middle of a calculation. Keep full accuracy and round at the end.
5. Answer the question that was asked: a length, a cost, a comparison or a decision, in the unit
the question used.
Rounding too early
A student computes 7 ÷ 3 ≈ 2.3, then multiplies by 3 and reports 6.9. The exact value is 7. Round
only when the value is reported, and keep the exact form in the working.
