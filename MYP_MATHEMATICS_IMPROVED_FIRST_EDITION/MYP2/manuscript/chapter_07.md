## 7.1 Angle relationships and parallel lines

**Criterion A · I · C · II** · Related concepts: Space, Relationships

> An angle is a measure of turn between two lines meeting at a point. A handful of facts about angles then let you find angles you never measured.

### 03 · ENGAGE

*Why a bridge is a lattice of triangles.*

[Photograph: ch07_bridge.jpg — A steel truss bridge. The diagonals lock each bay into triangles, so the span keeps its shape under load.]

### 04 · EXPLORE

*Measure, then explain.*

[Figure: parallel_transversal — Two parallel lines cut by a transversal. The eight angles formed fall into two equal groups, which is why one measurement determines all of them.]

6. Explain why a truss of triangles cannot be distorted by pushing on a corner, while a rectangle can.

### 05 · EXPLAIN

Angles on a straight line add to 180°. Angles at a point add to 360°. *Vertically opposite* angles are equal. When a transversal cuts two parallel lines, *corresponding* angles are equal, *alternate* angles are equal, and *co-interior* angles add to 180°.

| Fact | What it looks like | What you may write |
|---|---|---|
| Straight line | two angles side by side on a line | *a* + *b* = 180° |
| At a point | angles all the way round one vertex | *a* + *b* + *c* = 360° |
| Vertically opposite | an X shape | the two angles are equal |
| Corresponding (parallel) | same position at each intersection | the two angles are equal |
| Alternate (parallel) | opposite sides of the transversal, Z shape | the two angles are equal |
| Co-interior (parallel) | same side of the transversal, C shape | the two angles add to 180° |

**Worked example 7.1.1 — Two facts, one answer**

Two angles lie on a straight line. One is 118°. Find the other.

1. Angles on a straight line add to 180°.
2. So the other angle is 180° − 118° = 62°.
3. Check: 118 + 62 = 180, as required.

*Answer:* 62°

*Check:* Name the fact before you calculate; it is the first mark in an examination.

**Worked example 7.1.2 — Parallel lines**

A transversal cuts two parallel lines. One of the co-interior angles is 73°. Find the alternate angle to the 73° angle and the co-interior angle.

1. Co-interior angles add to 180°, so the other is 180° − 73° = 107°.
2. The alternate angle equals the 73° angle, so it is 73°.
3. Check: 73 + 107 = 180, and the alternate angle sits in the Z shape with the 73° angle.

*Answer:* Alternate 73°; co-interior 107°

*Check:* Sketch the Z and C shapes; the letters remind you which pair is which.

**Worked example 7.1.3 — Angles with algebra**

Two angles on a straight line are (3*x* + 10)° and (*x* + 30)°. Find *x* and both angles.

1. Angles on a straight line add to 180°: (3*x* + 10) + (*x* + 30) = 180.
2. Collect: 4*x* + 40 = 180, so 4*x* = 140 and *x* = 35.
3. The angles are 3(35) + 10 = 115° and 35 + 30 = 65°.
4. Check: 115 + 65 = 180.

*Answer:* *x* = 35; 115° and 65°

*Check:* Always substitute back: the check catches an algebra slip in one line.

“The answer is 62° because the two angles look equal.” Angles in a diagram are not drawn to scale, and equal-looking angles may be supplementary. Every angle must be justified by a named fact, not by appearance.

## 7.2 Triangles

**Criterion A · II · C · III** · Related concepts: Space, Generalisation

> A triangle is the simplest rigid polygon: fix its three sides and its shape cannot change. Its angles follow a single rule.

[Figure: triangle_angles]

Draw any triangle, tear off its three corners and place them together along a straight line: they fit exactly. That experiment is the reason for the angle sum, and the reason is the straight-line fact from section 7.1.

**Two results worth knowing by name**

**Angle sum of a triangle.** The three interior angles of any triangle add to 180°.
**Exterior angle.** An exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two interior opposite angles.

### Types of triangle

| Name | Property | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Equilateral | three equal sides | each angle is 60° |
| Isosceles | two equal sides | the angles opposite them are equal |
| Scalene | no equal sides | no equal angles |
| Right-angled | one angle of 90° | the other two add to 90° |

**Worked example 7.2.1 — Two angles given**

A triangle has angles of 42° and 67°. Find the third angle.

1. The angles of a triangle add to 180°.
2. Third angle = 180° − 42° − 67° = 71°.
3. Check: 42 + 67 + 71 = 180.

*Answer:* 71°

*Check:* Subtract both known angles in one step to avoid rounding twice.

**Worked example 7.2.2 — Isosceles triangle**

An isosceles triangle has an apex angle of 44°. Find the two base angles.

1. The base angles are equal because the sides opposite them are equal.
2. They share 180° − 44° = 136°.
3. Each base angle is 136° ÷ 2 = 68°.
4. Check: 44 + 68 + 68 = 180.

*Answer:* 68° each

*Check:* Divide after subtracting: the equal pair shares the remainder.

**Worked example 7.2.3 — The exterior angle**

The exterior angle at one vertex of a triangle is 125°. One interior opposite angle is 48°. Find the other interior opposite angle.

1. The exterior angle equals the sum of the two interior opposite angles.
2. So 125° = 48° + *y*.
3. *y* = 125° − 48° = 77°.
4. Check: the third interior angle is 180 − 48 − 77 = 55°, and 125 + 55 = 180 on a straight line.

*Answer:* 77°

*Check:* The exterior angle and the adjacent interior angle always add to 180°.

“The angles of a triangle are 90°, 45° and 55° because the diagram looks right.” Those three add to 190°, so no such triangle exists. Always add your three answers as a check; if the sum is not 180°, one of them is wrong.

## 7.3 Quadrilaterals and polygons

**Criterion B · I · A · III** · Related concepts: Patterns, Generalisation

> A polygon's angle sum is fixed by its number of sides. That single relationship replaces a page of separate facts.

[Figure: quadrilateral_row — Families of quadrilaterals. Each shape's angle and side properties follow from its definition, not from the way it is drawn.]

| Quadrilateral | Angle fact | Side fact |
|---|---|---|
| Parallelogram | opposite angles equal; all four add to 360° | opposite sides equal and parallel |
| Rhombus | opposite angles equal | all four sides equal |
| Rectangle | all four angles 90° | opposite sides equal |
| Square | all four angles 90° | all four sides equal |
| Trapezium | angles add to 360° | one pair of parallel sides |
| Kite | one pair of opposite angles equal | two pairs of adjacent equal sides |

[Figure: polygon_dissection — Dissecting a polygon from one vertex gives *n* − 2 triangles, so its interior angles sum to (*n* − 2) × 180°.]

**Interior angle sum** of a polygon with *n* sides: (*n* − 2) × 180°.
**Exterior angles** of any convex polygon add to 360°, so each exterior angle of a regular polygon is 360° ÷ *n*.
**Each interior angle** of a regular polygon is (*n* − 2) × 180° ÷ *n*.

**Worked example 7.3.1 — Quadrilateral and pentagon**

Find the missing angle of a quadrilateral whose other angles are 85°, 95° and 110°, then find each interior angle of a regular pentagon.

1. A quadrilateral's angles add to 360°, so the missing angle is 360 − 85 − 95 − 110 = 70°.
2. A pentagon has 5 sides, so its sum is (5 − 2) × 180 = 540°.
3. A regular pentagon divides this equally: 540 ÷ 5 = 108°.
4. Check with the exterior angle: 360 ÷ 5 = 72, and 180 − 72 = 108°.

*Answer:* 70°; 108°

*Check:* The exterior-angle check works for every regular polygon.

**Worked example 7.3.2 — How many sides?**

A regular polygon has an exterior angle of 24°. How many sides does it have, and what is each interior angle?

1. Exterior angles add to 360° and are equal in a regular polygon.
2. Number of sides = 360 ÷ 24 = 15.
3. Each interior angle = 180 − 24 = 156°.
4. Check: 15 × 156 = 2340, and (15 − 2) × 180 = 2340.

*Answer:* 15 sides; 156°

*Check:* Two independent routes — exterior angles and the interior sum — agreeing is a strong check.

**Worked example 7.3.3 — Is it possible?**

Can a regular polygon have an interior angle of 130°?

1. If the interior angle were 130°, the exterior angle would be 50°.
2. The number of sides would be 360 ÷ 50 = 7.2.
3. A polygon cannot have 7.2 sides, so no such regular polygon exists.
4. The angle must be one that divides 360 exactly when subtracted from 180°.

*Answer:* No — it would need 7.2 sides

*Check:* Test candidates with the exterior angle; a whole-number result is required.

“A regular polygon with 10 sides has each interior angle 180° ÷ 10 = 18°.” That is the exterior angle. Interior and exterior angles add to 180°, so the interior angle is 144°, and 10 × 144 = 1440 = (10 − 2) × 180.

## 7.4 Circles: circumference and area

**Criterion A · II · D · II** · Related concepts: Measurement, Models

> A circle is fixed by one length — its radius. Both its boundary and its interior follow from that single number.

[Figure: circle_parts — The parts of a circle. The ratio of circumference to diameter is π for every circle, whatever its size.]

**Circumference:** *C* = π*d* = 2π*r*.
**Area:** *A* = π*r*².
Use π = 3.14 unless the question says otherwise, and always state the unit: cm, cm², m².

The constant π is a ratio, not a measurement: for any circle, the distance round the edge is about 3.14 times the distance across the middle. The area formula comes from cutting the circle into thin sectors and rearranging them into a shape close to a parallelogram of base π*r* and height *r*.

**Worked example 7.4.1 — From diameter to area**

A circular table top has diameter 1.4 m. Find its circumference and area, using π = 3.14.

1. The radius is half the diameter: *r* = 0.7 m.
2. Circumference = 2π*r* = 2 × 3.14 × 0.7 = 4.396 m.
3. Area = π*r*² = 3.14 × 0.7² = 3.14 × 0.49 = 1.5386 m².
4. State units: circumference in metres, area in square metres.

*Answer:* 4.40 m and 1.54 m² (2 d.p.)

*Check:* Square the radius before multiplying by π; order matters.

**Worked example 7.4.2 — A semicircle**

A semicircle has radius 6 cm. Find its perimeter and area.

1. Area of the full circle is π × 6² = 113.04 cm²; half is 56.52 cm².
2. The curved edge of the semicircle is half the circumference: π × 6 = 18.84 cm.
3. The perimeter also includes the straight diameter, 12 cm.
4. Perimeter = 18.84 + 12 = 30.84 cm.

*Answer:* Area 56.52 cm²; perimeter 30.84 cm

*Check:* Perimeter follows the boundary, so the diameter must be included.

**Worked example 7.4.3 — Working backwards**

A circular flower bed has circumference 31.4 m. Find its radius and area.

1. *C* = 2π*r*, so *r* = *C* ÷ (2π) = 31.4 ÷ 6.28 = 5 m.
2. Area = π*r*² = 3.14 × 25 = 78.5 m².
3. Check: 2 × 3.14 × 5 = 31.4, the given circumference.

*Answer:* *r* = 5 m; area 78.5 m²

*Check:* Rearranging before substituting avoids arithmetic clutter.

“The area of a circle of diameter 10 cm is 3.14 × 10² = 314 cm².” The formula needs the radius, not the diameter: with *r* = 5 the area is 78.5 cm². A factor of four error is the classic circle mistake.

## 7.5 Composite shapes and perimeter

**Criterion C · I · D · III** · Related concepts: Measurement, Models

> Most real surfaces are not one shape. Splitting them into shapes you know is the core skill; deciding how to split them is the judgement.

[Figure: composite_area — A composite shape split into a rectangle and a semicircle. The dashed line shows one useful split; other splits give the same area.]

Areas add: the area of a composite shape is the sum of the areas of its parts, provided the parts do not overlap. Perimeters do not add in the same carefree way, because the boundary of a composite shape excludes the internal dividing line.

**Worked example 7.5.1 — Rectangle and semicircle**

A window is a rectangle 80 cm wide and 120 cm high with a semicircle on top. Find its total area and its perimeter, using π = 3.14.

1. Rectangle area = 80 × 120 = 9600 cm².
2. Semicircle radius = 40 cm, so its area = π × 40² ÷ 2 = 2512 cm².
3. Total area = 9600 + 2512 = 12 112 cm².
4. Perimeter = 120 + 80 + 120 + (π × 80 ÷ 2) = 320 + 125.6 = 445.6 cm.

*Answer:* Area 12 112 cm²; perimeter 445.6 cm

*Check:* The top edge of the rectangle is inside the shape, so it is not part of the perimeter.

**Worked example 7.5.2 — Subtracting a shape**

A square lawn of side 10 m has a circular pond of diameter 6 m in the middle. Find the grassed area.

1. Lawn area = 10 × 10 = 100 m².
2. Pond radius = 3 m, so pond area = 3.14 × 9 = 28.26 m².
3. Grassed area = 100 − 28.26 = 71.74 m².
4. Check that the pond fits inside the square: diameter 6 m < 10 m.

*Answer:* 71.74 m²

*Check:* Subtracting is splitting with a negative sign; keep the units consistent.

**Worked example 7.5.3 — A shape with a missing corner**

An L-shaped room is a 6 m by 5 m rectangle with a 2 m by 3 m rectangle removed from one corner. Find its area and perimeter.

1. Whole rectangle area = 6 × 5 = 30 m².
2. Removed area = 2 × 3 = 6 m², so the room is 24 m².
3. Boundary: 6 + 5 + 4 + 2 + 2 + 3 = 22 m, walking round the L.
4. Check the boundary by adding only outer edges, skipping the internal lines.

*Answer:* 24 m²; perimeter 22 m

*Check:* Walk the boundary once, in order, and add each edge as you pass it.

“The perimeter of the composite shape is the perimeter of the rectangle plus the circumference of the circle.” Parts of the boundary are internal, and some circle edges are only partly exposed. Trace the actual outline of the finished shape before adding anything.

## 7.6 Summary, revision and reflection

### What this chapter established

- Angles on a straight line add to 180°; angles at a point add to 360°; vertically opposite angles are equal.
- Parallel lines give equal corresponding and alternate angles; co-interior angles add to 180°.
- The angles of a triangle add to 180°, and an exterior angle equals the sum of the two interior opposite angles.
- The interior angles of an *n*-sided polygon add to (*n* − 2) × 180°; exterior angles add to 360°.
- *C* = 2π*r* and *A* = π*r*²; the radius, not the diameter, belongs in both.
- Composite areas add, but perimeters must be traced along the real boundary.

**Vocabulary to use precisely**

**acute** · **obtuse** · **reflex** · **vertically opposite** · **corresponding** · **alternate** · **co-interior** · **isosceles** · **exterior angle** · **regular polygon** · **circumference** · **composite shape**

### Misconceptions to close

| The mistake | What is really happening |
|---|---|
| Judging angles by appearance. | Diagrams are not to scale; only named facts justify an answer. |
| Using the diameter in the area formula. | Area needs the radius; halving the diameter first changes the answer by a factor of 4. |
| Adding perimeters of joined shapes. | Internal edges are not part of the boundary and must be removed. |
| Writing 18° for a decagon's interior angle. | 18° is the exterior angle; the interior angle is 144°. |
