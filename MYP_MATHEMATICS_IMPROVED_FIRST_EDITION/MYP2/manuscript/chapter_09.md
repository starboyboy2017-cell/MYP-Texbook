# Chapter 9 · Transformations and symmetry

*Part III · Geometry and space* · key concept **Relationships** · related concepts: Patterns, Movement

Global context: Personal and cultural expression

A transformation moves a shape without arguing about it:
every point obeys the same rule. Symmetry is what you get
when the rule leaves the shape looking unchanged.

### 01 · MYP UNIT PLANNER

*Four moves, one coordinate grid, and the patterns they generate.*

**Statement of inquiry.** A small set of transformations generates every repeating pattern, so the symmetry of a design can be described as precisely as its geometry.

**Key and related concepts**

**Key concept · Relationships.** Each transformation is a rule linking every point to exactly one image point.
**Related · Patterns.** Repetition is the visible result of a transformation applied again and again.
**Related · Movement.** Translation, reflection, rotation and enlargement are the four ways a figure can be moved.

**Global context · Personal and cultural expression**

Exploration: symmetry in textile design, mosaic and architecture. A pattern is a cultural statement made with mathematical operations.

## 9.1 Translation

**Criterion A · I · C · II** · Related concepts: Movement, Patterns

> A translation slides a shape without turning it. Every point moves by the same amount in the same direction, so the shape's size and orientation are unchanged.

*Move, record, compare.*

### 05 · EXPLAIN

A *translation* moves every point of a shape the same distance in the same direction. It is described by a *column vector* ( *a* over *b* ): *a* units right and *b* units up, with negative values meaning left and down. A translation produces a *congruent* image.

**Worked example 9.1.1 — Translate a point**

Translate the point A(2, 3) by the vector (3, −1). State the image.

1. A move of 3 right adds 3 to the *x*-coordinate: 2 + 3 = 5.
2. A move of 1 down subtracts 1 from the *y*-coordinate: 3 − 1 = 2.
3. The image is A′(5, 2).
4. Check the direction: the new point is right and below the original.

*Answer:* A′(5, 2)

*Check:* Add the vector to the coordinates; do not multiply.

**Worked example 9.1.2 — Translate a triangle**

A triangle has vertices (1, 1), (4, 1) and (1, 3). Translate it by the vector (2, 3).

1. Apply the same vector to each vertex.
2. (1, 1) → (3, 4); (4, 1) → (6, 4); (1, 3) → (3, 6).
3. The image triangle has vertices (3, 4), (6, 4) and (3, 6).
4. Check: each image vertex is 2 right and 3 above its object vertex.

*Answer:* (3, 4), (6, 4), (3, 6)

*Check:* A translation keeps side lengths and angles, so the image is congruent.

**Worked example 9.1.3 — Find the vector**

A translation maps (2, 5) to (2, 1). Find the vector and the image of (7, 3).

1. The *x*-coordinate does not change: the horizontal component is 0.
2. The *y*-coordinate falls from 5 to 1: the vertical component is −4.
3. The vector is (0, −4).
4. Applying it to (7, 3) gives (7, −1).

*Answer:* (0, −4); image (7, −1)

*Check:* Subtract the object coordinates from the image coordinates to find the vector.

“A translation changes the size of the shape if the vector is long.” A translation only moves a shape; it never stretches it. The vector describes distance and direction, not scale.

## 9.2 Reflection and line symmetry

**Criterion A · II · B · I** · Related concepts: Patterns, Form

> A reflection flips a shape across a mirror line. Distances from the line are preserved, but the shape's orientation is reversed.

[Figure: reflection — A reflection in the line *y* = *x*. Each point and its image are the same distance from the mirror line, measured perpendicular to it.]

[Figure: symmetry_shapes — Lines and orders of symmetry for regular polygons. A regular *n*-gon has *n* lines of symmetry and rotational symmetry of order *n*.]

| Mirror line | Rule for the image of (*x*, *y*) |
|---|---|
| the *x*-axis | (*x*, −*y*) |
| the *y*-axis | (−*x*, *y*) |
| the line *y* = *x* | (*y*, *x*) |
| the line *y* = −*x* | (−*y*, −*x*) |

A *line of symmetry* divides a shape into two halves that are mirror images. A shape's *order of rotational symmetry* is the number of positions in a full turn in which it looks unchanged. A circle has infinitely many lines of symmetry.

**Worked example 9.2.1 — Reflect a point**

Reflect (3, 2) in (a) the *x*-axis, (b) the *y*-axis and (c) the line *y* = *x*.

1. (a) The *x*-axis flips vertically: (3, −2).
2. (b) The *y*-axis flips horizontally: (−3, 2).
3. (c) In *y* = *x* the coordinates swap: (2, 3).
4. Check (c): the line *y* = *x* is the diagonal through (0, 0) and (1, 1).

*Answer:* (3, −2), (−3, 2) and (2, 3)

*Check:* Sketch the point and the mirror line before applying the rule.

**Worked example 9.2.2 — Reflect a triangle**

A triangle has vertices (1, 1), (3, 1) and (2, 4). Reflect it in the *x*-axis.

1. Reflecting in the *x*-axis sends (*x*, *y*) to (*x*, −*y*).
2. (1, 1) → (1, −1); (3, 1) → (3, −1); (2, 4) → (2, −4).
3. The image triangle is below the axis, with the same side lengths.
4. The image is congruent but has been flipped: clockwise has become anticlockwise.

*Answer:* (1, −1), (3, −1), (2, −4)

*Check:* All three vertices must move; a reflection is determined by its mirror line.

**Worked example 9.2.3 — Symmetry of a shape**

How many lines of symmetry and what order of rotational symmetry does (a) a square and (b) a regular hexagon have?

1. (a) A square has 4 lines of symmetry: one vertical, one horizontal and two diagonals.
2. Its rotational symmetry is of order 4, because a quarter turn returns it to itself.
3. (b) A regular hexagon has 6 lines of symmetry and rotational symmetry of order 6.
4. For a regular polygon both numbers equal the number of sides.

*Answer:* (a) 4 lines, order 4 (b) 6 lines, order 6

*Check:* For a regular polygon, lines of symmetry = order of rotational symmetry = number of sides.

“Every quadrilateral has four lines of symmetry.” Only a square does. A rectangle has two, a rhombus two, a parallelogram none — and a kite has one. Symmetry depends on the shape's actual proportions, not its name.

## 9.3 Rotation and rotational symmetry

**Criterion A · III · C · III** · Related concepts: Movement, Space

> A rotation turns a shape about a fixed point. The centre, the angle and the direction together determine the image completely.

[Figure: rotation — A rotation of 90° anticlockwise about the origin. Distances from the centre are preserved; only direction changes.]

| Rotation about the origin | Rule for (*x*, *y*) |
|---|---|
| 90° clockwise | (*y*, −*x*) |
| 90° anticlockwise | (−*y*, *x*) |
| 180° (either direction) | (−*x*, −*y*) |

[Photograph: ch09_wheel.jpg — A Ferris wheel. Each cabin is carried through the same rotation about the hub, so the wheel's rotational symmetry is of high order.]

A *rotation* turns a shape about a *centre* through a given *angle*, either clockwise or anticlockwise. Rotations preserve lengths and angles but change the direction a shape faces.

**Worked example 9.3.1 — Rotate a point**

Rotate the point (3, 0) about the origin by (a) 90° clockwise and (b) 180°.

1. (a) 90° clockwise sends (*x*, *y*) to (*y*, −*x*): (0, −3).
2. (b) 180° sends (*x*, *y*) to (−*x*, −*y*): (−3, 0).
3. Check: the distance from the origin is 3 in every case.
4. The point has moved round the origin, not towards or away from it.

*Answer:* (0, −3) and (−3, 0)

*Check:* The distance to the centre never changes under rotation, which is a quick check.

**Worked example 9.3.2 — Find the rotation**

A rotation about the origin maps (5, 0) to (0, 5). Describe it.

1. The image is on the *y*-axis, so the turn is a quarter turn.
2. Moving from the positive *x*-axis to the positive *y*-axis is anticlockwise.
3. The rotation is 90° anticlockwise about the origin.
4. Check with the rule: (−*y*, *x*) sends (5, 0) to (0, 5).

*Answer:* 90° anticlockwise about the origin

*Check:* A quarter turn clockwise would send (5, 0) to (0, −5).

**Worked example 9.3.3 — Order of rotational symmetry**

What is the order of rotational symmetry of a regular pentagon, and of the letter S?

1. A regular pentagon looks unchanged after each 360° ÷ 5 = 72° turn.
2. It returns to itself five times in a full turn, so the order is 5.
3. The letter S is unchanged after a half turn, so its order is 2.
4. A shape with no rotational symmetry other than 360° has order 1.

*Answer:* Pentagon 5; letter S 2

*Check:* Order counts the positions in a full turn where the shape looks the same, including the start.

“A rotation changes the size of a shape, because the far corners travel further.” Points further from the centre do move further, but every distance within the shape is unchanged, so the image is congruent. Only an enlargement changes size.

## 9.4 Enlargement

**Criterion A · IV · D · III** · Related concepts: Movement, Measurement

> An enlargement changes size by a scale factor from a fixed centre. It is the only one of the four transformations that changes lengths.

[Figure: enlargement — An enlargement with scale factor 3 from a centre at one corner. Every distance from the centre triples, so lengths multiply by 3 and areas by 9.]

An *enlargement* with centre C and *scale factor* *k* sends each point to a point *k* times as far from C, on the same line. Lengths multiply by *k*, areas by *k*², volumes by *k*³. A negative *k* places the image on the opposite side of the centre.

**Worked example 9.4.1 — Enlarge from the origin**

Enlarge the triangle with vertices (1, 1), (2, 1) and (1, 2) by scale factor 3 from the origin.

1. Multiply each coordinate by 3.
2. (1, 1) → (3, 3); (2, 1) → (6, 3); (1, 2) → (3, 6).
3. The image triangle is three times as far from the origin in both directions.
4. Check a side length: 1 unit becomes 3 units, as the scale factor requires.

*Answer:* (3, 3), (6, 3), (3, 6)

*Check:* For a centre at the origin, an enlargement is multiplication of both coordinates.

**Worked example 9.4.2 — Centre not at the origin**

Enlarge the point (2, 3) by scale factor 2 from the centre (1, 1).

1. Find the vector from the centre to the point: (2 − 1, 3 − 1) = (1, 2).
2. Multiply it by the scale factor: 2 × (1, 2) = (2, 4).
3. Add it back to the centre: (1 + 2, 1 + 4) = (3, 5).
4. Check: the image is twice as far from (1, 1) as the original.

*Answer:* (3, 5)

*Check:* Subtract the centre first, multiply, then add the centre back.

**Worked example 9.4.3 — Area and scale factor**

A photograph 6 cm by 4 cm is enlarged so that its longer side becomes 15 cm. Find the scale factor and the area of the enlargement.

1. Scale factor = 15 ÷ 6 = 2.5.
2. The shorter side becomes 4 × 2.5 = 10 cm.
3. Area of the original is 24 cm²; the enlargement is 15 × 10 = 150 cm².
4. Check with the area scale factor: 24 × 2.5² = 24 × 6.25 = 150 cm².

*Answer:* *k* = 2.5; area 150 cm²

*Check:* Areas follow *k*², so an area check is a strong test of a scale factor.

“A shape enlarged by scale factor 2 has twice the area.” Both lengths double, so the area multiplies by 2 × 2 = 4. A 3 cm square becomes 6 cm and its area grows from 9 to 36 cm².

## 9.5 Tessellation and symmetry in design

**Criterion B · II · D · IV** · Related concepts: Patterns, Relationships

> A tessellation covers the plane with no gaps and no overlaps. Which shapes can do it is decided by the angles at a point, and the design is decided by the transformations used.

[Figure: tessellation — A tessellation built by translation and rotation. Each copy is congruent to the original tile.]

[Photograph: ch09_mosaic.jpg — Mosaic tiles laid on a diagonal grid. The pattern is generated by translations along two directions, with reflections across the seams.]

Three regular hexagons meet at a vertex because 3 × 120° = 360°. Four squares meet because 4 × 90° = 360°. A regular pentagon cannot tile on its own because 108° does not divide 360° exactly — three pentagons leave a gap of 36°.

**Worked example 9.5.1 — Why a shape tiles**

Explain why a regular octagon does not tessellate on its own.

1. Each interior angle of a regular octagon is (8 − 2) × 180 ÷ 8 = 135°.
2. At a point the angles must total 360°: 360 ÷ 135 = 2.67.
3. Two octagons give 270°, leaving a 90° gap; three give 405°, which overlaps.
4. So octagons need another shape — a square fits the 90° gap, which is why octagons and squares tile together.

*Answer:* 135° does not divide 360°

*Check:* The test is always: does the interior angle divide 360 exactly?

**Worked example 9.5.2 — Describing a pattern**

A border design repeats a single motif every 6 cm along a strip, with each motif also reflected in the centre line. Name the transformations used.

1. Repeating along the strip is a translation by 6 cm each time.
2. The reflection across the centre line creates the mirror version.
3. The two operations together give a pattern with translation symmetry and a line of symmetry.
4. No rotation or enlargement is needed to describe the design.

*Answer:* Translation and reflection

*Check:* A complete description names the vector and the mirror line, not only the types.

**Worked example 9.5.3 — Symmetry of a logo**

A logo is a regular hexagon divided into six identical triangles, three shaded and three white, alternating. State its symmetry.

1. The hexagon returns to itself after every 60° turn, so the order of rotational symmetry is 6.
2. Alternating shading also returns after each 60°, because shading is unchanged by another sixth of a turn.
3. The three lines joining opposite vertices are lines of symmetry.
4. The three lines joining midpoints of opposite sides are not, because they swap shaded with white.

*Answer:* Order 6; three lines of symmetry

*Check:* Check symmetry on the colouring, not only on the outline.

“Any shape that fits around a point tiles the plane.” Meeting at one point is not enough: the arrangement must continue across the whole plane. Pentagons of the right kind can tile, but regular pentagons cannot.

## 9.6 Summary, revision and reflection

### What this chapter established

- A translation slides by a column vector and leaves size and orientation unchanged.
- A reflection flips across a mirror line; distances from the line are preserved but orientation is reversed.
- A rotation turns about a centre through an angle in a stated direction.
- An enlargement multiplies lengths by *k*, areas by *k*² and volumes by *k*³.
- Regular polygons have as many lines of symmetry as sides, and rotational symmetry of the same order.
- A shape tiles the plane when its angles fit exactly round a point and the arrangement continues without gaps.

**Vocabulary to use precisely**

**translation** · **column vector** · **reflection** · **mirror line** · **rotation** · **centre of rotation** · **enlargement** · **scale factor** · **congruent** · **similar** · **tessellation** · **order of symmetry**

### Misconceptions to close

| The mistake | What is really happening |
|---|---|
| Giving a transformation's type but no details. | Every transformation needs its vector, line, centre and angle, or centre and scale factor. |
| Thinking an enlargement of scale factor 2 doubles the area. | Area scales by *k*², so it quadruples. |
| Assuming a parallelogram has lines of symmetry. | It has none: a half turn maps it to itself, but no mirror does. |
| Believing a long translation vector stretches the shape. | A translation never changes size; only enlargement does. |
